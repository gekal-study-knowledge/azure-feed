### Adding network intelligence into a network-ready Azure migration plan

- **Link**: [https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/adding-network-intelligence-into-a-network-ready-azure-migration/ba-p/4554519](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/adding-network-intelligence-into-a-network-ready-azure-migration/ba-p/4554519)
- **Published**: 2026-09-10 03:01:43
- **Fetched**: 2026-09-10 15:43:47

<details class="entry-summary">
<summary>詳細を表示</summary>
<pre><span class="lia-text-color-21"><em><strong>Sudha Mahajan, Partner PM Azure Networking, Vijay Mamtani, CVP Azure Networking | September 2026</strong></em></span></pre>
<p><span class="lia-text-color-15"><strong>Lift-and-shift succeeds when the network moves with the application&nbsp;</strong></span></p>
<p><span>For many VMware customers, the immediate migration goal is practical: move workloads to Azure quickly, preserve application behavior and connectivity, reduce execution risk, keep it secure, and build a costed plan that leaders can approve. Compute and storage sizing are necessary, but it is not the whole migration plan. Applications depend on IP prefixes, network paths, security policies, load-balancing behavior, shared services, and connections to systems that may move at different times.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Azure Migrate is critical to making this work manageable. It provides the foundation for discovering the estate, assessing migration readiness, developing the business case, and translating collected evidence into a plan. With Network Planning, that foundation becomes network-aware, combining server inventory with insights into how application connectivity can be represented in Azure.</span></p>
<p><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><strong><span class="lia-text-color-15">Networking is the hidden critical path&nbsp;</span></strong></p>
<p><span>Network planning often begins late. A team may group and size virtual machines, then assume the target network can be designed near cutover. That approach overlooks the dependencies that determine whether an application will function after migration.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The required information for networking is fragmented. Workload inventory may sit in vCenter; network constructs and flows may be reflected in NSX; policy intent may be distributed across firewalls; application delivery may depend on load balancers; address constraints may live in spreadsheets; and operational knowledge may be divided among application, infrastructure, security, and network teams. Each system provides part of the answer, but no single view naturally explains what an application needs in Azure.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Common mistakes follow - planning by individual servers rather than application, missing dependencies, carrying forward rules without understanding their intent, overlooking shared services, estimating compute while omitting network costs, or designing target topology before the source environment is fully understood. These gaps create additional analysis, redesign, stakeholder review iterations, and cutover uncertainty. Networking becomes the hidden critical path - not because teams lack expertise, but because the evidence needed for decisions is incomplete and disconnected.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><span class="lia-text-color-15"><strong>Network planning bridges discovery and decisions&nbsp;</strong></span></p>
<p><span>Network planning is designed to close that gap within the Azure Migrate. Its purpose is to turn discovered network evidence and customer migration intent into an actionable, Azure-oriented planning view.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Beginning September, Azure migrate enters public preview with network-aware assessment and networking incorporated into business-case and assessment functionality.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Visualize and validate source network resources:</span><span> Discovered VMware network information provides context for workload placement, connectivity, dependencies, and applicable destination constructs.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Azure-native recommendations:</span><span> Network Planning translates source findings into recommended Azure networking building blocks and guidance, including topology considerations (hub-and-spoke), security intent considerations (mapping on-prem firewall rules to NSG and Azure FW) and application delivery considerations (Application load balancing and WAF rules), instead of requiring customers to manually map every source construct.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Readiness and issue visibility:</span><span> Assessments surface network-related readiness considerations and planning issues that require attention before migration, allowing teams to separate straightforward moves from items needing design decisions or remediation.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Networking in the business case:</span><span> Network-cost estimates are included alongside the broader migration analysis, helping decision-makers evaluate a more complete cost picture.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Security-rule planning context:</span><span> Where source data supports it, translated security-rule information can help teams reason about security requirements and prepare for target policy design and validation.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>

<p><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span class="lia-text-color-15"><strong>What changes for customers and partners&nbsp;</strong></span></p>
<p><span>Network planning does not eliminate the need for engineering judgment or automate network design. Its value is a more structured starting point, grounded in discovered data and presented in the same Azure Migrate workflow used for assessment and business-case development.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Customers can bring application, network, security, and migration stakeholders to a shared planning view. They can identify missing information sooner, connect network requirements to migration groups, review readiness before cutover planning, and include network considerations in financial discussions. Partners can use the same evidence to guide workshops, validate assumptions, focus design effort on exceptions, and produce a plan that is easier for customer teams to review.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The result is a more practical migration conversation: not simply "Which virtual machines can move?" but "What does this application require, what must be addressed, what Azure is the recommended network approach , and what should be included in the costed plan?"</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><span class="lia-text-color-15"><strong>Launch scope first, roadmap second&nbsp;</strong></span></p>
<p><span>The August preview of network planning in Azure Migrate is intentionally focused on the lift-and-shift planning path. Items such as application modernization, third-party virtual appliances, advanced topologies, infrastructure-as-code generation, and IP-retention scenarios remain future roadmap areas. Keeping that boundary clear allows customers to evaluate the preview for what it delivers now while Microsoft continues to shape later capabilities from feedback.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><span class="lia-text-color-15"><strong>Key takeaway&nbsp;</strong></span></p>
<p><span>For customers preparing VMware lift-and-shift migrations, begin by enabling and validating discovery in Azure Migrate, then choose a representative application or migration group. Review its workloads, dependencies, source network context, readiness findings, Azure recommendations, and estimated network costs with the teams responsible for application ownership, networking, security, and migration execution.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>For partners, use Network planning to anchor planning conversations in evidence rather than assumptions and to connect technical findings directly to the business case.&nbsp;</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>A successful migration plan should describe more than where servers will run. It should explain how applications will remain connected, what issues must be resolved, what the target approach will require, and what it is expected to cost. Network planning brings those networks decisions into Azure Migrate - where lift-and-shift planning already begins.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p>Cloud operations are most effective when teams can turn insights into understanding and understanding into action. Whether you’re deploying new services, troubleshooting an issue, assessing resource health, or managing change, you need operational intelligence that helps you make faster, more informed decisions and continuously improve your environment.</p>
<p>This is the promise of agentic cloud operations, a new operating model that uses AI powered agents equipped with deep contextual understanding and built in governance to help you run your cloud environments with speed and confidence. <a href="https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/products/copilot" target="_blank">Azure Copilot</a> brings AI-powered operations directly into Azure, helping you understand your environment, identify issues, determine likely causes and take informed action using the context already available in your Azure resources and services.</p>
<p>Today, Azure Copilot is announcing <strong>the general availability of Troubleshooting Agent</strong>, a unified, built-in Azure Copilot capability that helps customers investigate and resolve operational issues faster. Available through both Azure Copilot and Support + Troubleshooting in the Azure portal, Troubleshooting Agent enables you to move from issue detection to resolution faster by bringing together troubleshooting insights, operational context, and recommended actions in a single experience.</p>
<h2>Deep expertise across Azure services</h2>
<p>Azure services support a wide variety of architectures, operational patterns, and workload requirements. While Azure Copilot provides consistent agentic troubleshooting across Azure, deeper service integrations enable specialized expertise and investigative capabilities for specific services.</p>
<p>At general availability, Azure Compute and Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS) are the first services to offer these enhanced troubleshooting experiences.</p>
<ul>
<li>Azure Compute: You can investigate issues such as unexpected virtual machine restarts, connectivity and boot failures, high resource utilization, deployment and allocation failures, and unhealthy scale set instances.</li>
<li>AKS: You can investigate issues such as pod restarts, stalled deployments, scheduling constraints, cluster networking, scaling behavior, upgrade regressions, and degraded performance.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>“This [Troubleshooting Agent] is really helpful for the person who is debugging, and they can come to a conclusion. Instead of going through the entire documentation, it is giving us the exact information on what it needs and what to look on.” – Senior Software Engineer, Lavelle Networks</em></p>
</blockquote>
<p>As the agentic troubleshooting capabilities of Azure Copilot continue to evolve, additional service specific skills and capabilities will be added to support a broader range of operational scenarios across Azure services through the same conversational experience.</p>
<h2>Grounded in Azure context, with customers in control</h2>
<p>Azure Copilot Troubleshooting Agent uses the resource information and supported diagnostics available for the affected resource while respecting your identity and Azure role based access control (RBAC).</p>
<p>The agent explains the information behind its findings and recommends actions for review, allowing you to remain in control of remediation actions. Diagnostic depth varies by service, resource type, and scenario, but the goal remains consistent: helping you investigate, diagnose, and resolve operational issues more effectively using the context already available within your Azure environment.</p>
<p>When additional assistance is needed, Azure Support remains available, but you can begin those engagements with a richer understanding of the issue, its likely causes, and potential remediation options.</p>
<h2>Available at no additional cost</h2>
<p>Troubleshooting Agent in Azure Copilot is available at no additional cost. There is no separate license or per-query charge, and existing Azure Support plans remain unchanged. Standard charges for the Azure resources continue to apply.</p>
<h2>Get started</h2>
<p>Open&nbsp;Azure Copilot and select Troubleshooting Agent&nbsp;in the <a class="lia-external-url" href="https://portal.azure.com/" target="_blank">Azure portal</a> or go to&nbsp;Support + Troubleshooting, then describe the issue you are experiencing. Include the affected resource or subscription if it is not already clear from your current context.</p>
<p>To get started, here are some prompt suggestions: <em>"Why did my virtual machine restart unexpectedly?", "Investigate why my AKS deployment is failing.", "Why can't I connect to this resource?", "What recent platform issues have affected this resource?", "Check whether this resource has active errors or configuration issues."</em></p>
<p>Troubleshooting Agent will use the supported resource diagnostics available for the investigation, explain what it finds, and recommend next steps.</p>
<h2>Looking ahead</h2>
<p>Connected cloud environments need a more intelligent approach to operations. As part of our broader vision for agentic cloud operations, Azure Copilot is your starting point when you need to understand, troubleshoot, and take action across your Azure environment. Troubleshooting Agent is an important step in that journey. Based on available context and diagnostics, we help you move from symptoms to informed next steps faster.</p>

<p><span><span><strong>Authors: </strong><a class="lia-mention lia-mention-user" href="">ebhattad​</a><strong>, </strong><a class="lia-mention lia-mention-user" href="">jenniferjhan​</a><strong>, </strong><a class="lia-mention lia-mention-user" href="">lakshminarasimha​</a><strong>, </strong><a class="lia-mention lia-mention-user" href="">bharadwajr​</a><strong>&nbsp;</strong></span></span></p>
<h3><span><span>The Challenge: Synthesizing fragmented feedback signals, to improve Azure's experience quality at scale</span></span></h3>
<p><span>Customers experience products and services end to end, but product&nbsp;experiences&nbsp;are often structured around individual service.&nbsp;One&nbsp;team may&nbsp;only&nbsp;know its top issues, while&nbsp;another may see only its own slice of&nbsp;experience.&nbsp;&nbsp;That structure&nbsp;makes&nbsp;it difficult to&nbsp;identify&nbsp;cross-cutting friction across the broader&nbsp;product&nbsp;experience.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The&nbsp;feedback&nbsp;signals themselves&nbsp;are&nbsp;also fragmented. Customers share feedback through in-product surveys, support cases, field conversations, and social channels. Most product teams&nbsp;can&nbsp;see only part of that picture, making it hard to distinguish&nbsp;isolated&nbsp;comments&nbsp;from meaningful trends,&nbsp;understand which issues were having the greatest impact, and&nbsp;avoid missing&nbsp;critical feedback.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>For the Azure&nbsp;team,&nbsp;the challenges of&nbsp;fragmentation&nbsp;were amplified&nbsp;by scale.&nbsp;The&nbsp;team processed&nbsp;roughly 10,000&nbsp;to 15,000 customer feedback reports each&nbsp;month, and&nbsp;synthesizing that feedback required&nbsp;80 to&nbsp;100 hours&nbsp;of expert analysis.&nbsp;&nbsp;Additional&nbsp;effort was needed&nbsp;to translate findings into consistent engineering&nbsp;work&nbsp;items.&nbsp;As feedback&nbsp;volume&nbsp;grew,&nbsp;manual analysis&nbsp;became increasingly&nbsp;unsustainable&nbsp;creating&nbsp;delays in identifying and addressing customer priorities.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Compounding&nbsp;the challenge was the absence of an&nbsp;effective&nbsp;feedback loop&nbsp;to measure the impact of&nbsp;quality improvements.&nbsp;Teams struggled to&nbsp;justify&nbsp;investments in quality over&nbsp;new features&nbsp;because the return on those investments was difficult to quantify.&nbsp;The absence of a closed-loop measurement system made it difficult to consistently assess the&nbsp;customer&nbsp;impact of quality improvements.&nbsp;</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The team needed a system that could&nbsp;operate&nbsp;across organizational boundaries and across the product development lifecycle:</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<ul>
<li><span>Identify&nbsp;the&nbsp;most&nbsp;critical&nbsp;customer issues across&nbsp;fragmented&nbsp;feedback channels and product areas.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><span>Convert&nbsp;those insights into&nbsp;actionable&nbsp;engineering work,&nbsp;help teams&nbsp;address&nbsp;issues&nbsp;effectively, and&nbsp;measure outcomes&nbsp;to close the loop.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span>The&nbsp;solution&nbsp;needed&nbsp;to preserve team-specific context,&nbsp;maintain&nbsp;auditability,&nbsp;continuously&nbsp;improve through feedback, and keep human experts in control of decisions that require judgment.&nbsp;To address these challenges, Microsoft launched the Great Experiences Matter (GEM)&nbsp;initiative.&nbsp;GEM is designed to analyze feedback signals in aggregate,&nbsp;with access controls and privacy safeguards designed to limit exposure&nbsp;of&nbsp;customer-identifiable information while helping teams&nbsp;identify&nbsp;patterns across channels.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<h3><span><span>The Solution: an agentic feedback-to-fix loop with humans in control</span></span><span>&nbsp;</span></h3>
<p><span>GEM created an AI-enabled&nbsp;feedback-to-fix&nbsp;workflow that connects customer listening, engineering action, and&nbsp;impact measurement across the product development lifecycle. The workflow uses&nbsp;Microsoft&nbsp;Foundry,&nbsp;Azure Data Explorer, Microsoft&nbsp;Fabric, Azure DevOps, and a set of custom agents to transform large volumes of qualitative feedback into prioritized insights and actionable&nbsp;engineering&nbsp;work.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<img />
<pre><span>Fig 1. GEM AI-enabled automation workflow</span><span>&nbsp;</span></pre>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><span>The&nbsp;system closes&nbsp;the loop through&nbsp;two&nbsp;connected motions.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span><strong>Find</strong>.</span><span>&nbsp;Agentic workflows remove noise and duplicate&nbsp;reports, assess relevance and actionability,&nbsp;classify&nbsp;feedback against known issues from UX research,&nbsp;cluster related&nbsp;issues, and&nbsp;surface&nbsp;likely&nbsp;root&nbsp;causes.&nbsp;GEM&nbsp;builds on&nbsp;years of deep end-to-end UX research that has&nbsp;identified&nbsp;systemic friction across customer journeys and product boundaries. By continuously triangulating GEM signals with ongoing research, we combine broad, scalable listening with deep human insight to inform a more cohesive Azure experience.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The results&nbsp;are surfaced&nbsp;through global&nbsp;scorecards&nbsp;for cross-cutting Azure issues and vertical scorecards tailored to individual product teams.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<img />
<pre><span>Fig 2.&nbsp;GEM Global scorecard of top issues&nbsp;with Azure, data has been fictionalized to protect intellectual property</span><span>&nbsp;</span></pre>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><span><strong>Fix</strong>.</span><span>&nbsp;The workflow creates Azure DevOps work items with the customer&nbsp;context,&nbsp;likely reproduction&nbsp;steps, recommended next actions, and an auditable&nbsp;trace of the&nbsp;supporting&nbsp;analysis.&nbsp;To date,&nbsp;42% of the&nbsp;identified&nbsp;issues have been addressed&nbsp;through engineering action.&nbsp;The team is also extending&nbsp;an AI-assisted&nbsp;engineering&nbsp;workflow,&nbsp;using GitHub Copilot&nbsp;cloud agent,&nbsp;that can generate proposed fixes&nbsp;for&nbsp;straightforward issues. Engineers&nbsp;remain&nbsp;responsible for reviewing, refining, approving, and&nbsp;shipping any changes.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The architecture is designed for inspection rather than blind automation.&nbsp;The recommendations&nbsp;include an&nbsp;auditable evidence trail&nbsp;allowing&nbsp;reviewers&nbsp;to&nbsp;inspect the&nbsp;source&nbsp;feedback, classifications, supporting references, confidence indicators, and recommendation&nbsp;actions.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Human&nbsp;expertise&nbsp;enters the system at several points. Researchers shape the&nbsp;issue&nbsp;taxonomies and qualitative grounding. Product teams define&nbsp;ownership boundaries,&nbsp;business priorities,&nbsp;domain-specific&nbsp;vocabulary, and&nbsp;trusted&nbsp;sources&nbsp;that guide agent analysis. Engineers review and act on resulting work items, while leaders use scorecards to&nbsp;inform&nbsp;investment decisions.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>This context is captured in configuration files that&nbsp;evolve alongside&nbsp;the&nbsp;products&nbsp;they support.&nbsp;Teams&nbsp;can add new issue categories, refine keywords, clarify ownership boundaries, or&nbsp;identify&nbsp;trusted research&nbsp;sources. The next analysis&nbsp;cycle&nbsp;automatically&nbsp;incorporates&nbsp;&nbsp;the&nbsp;updated context without requiring&nbsp;changes to the underlying agents.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>That design creates a "feedback&nbsp;loop&nbsp;for&nbsp;the feedback&nbsp;loop".&nbsp;Teams review the root-cause analyses and work-item quality,&nbsp;identify&nbsp;gaps, and refine their configurations.&nbsp;This&nbsp;enables&nbsp;teams to continuously&nbsp;embed domain&nbsp;expertise&nbsp;into the workflow, improving how&nbsp;feedback&nbsp;is&nbsp;interpreted and prioritized&nbsp;without requiring changes to the underlying infrastructure.&nbsp;Their input improves&nbsp;subsequent&nbsp;runs, helping the system become more precise while preserving local product knowledge.</span>&nbsp;<br />&nbsp;<br /><span>The agent also&nbsp;maintains&nbsp;access to reports from&nbsp;previous&nbsp;runs and uses tools such as Web IQ and MCP servers to assess whether previously&nbsp;identified&nbsp;issues are improving, still require attention, or can be confidently closed.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p><span>One&nbsp;demonstrated&nbsp;example surfaced customer reports that a networking tool lacked IPv6 validation and support. The workflow generated an engineering work item&nbsp;describing the issue,&nbsp;customer impact,&nbsp;likely&nbsp;reproduction&nbsp;path, and recommended&nbsp;actions. The networking team reproduced the issue,&nbsp;validated&nbsp;the finding, and added it to its backlog.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The goal is not to remove people from the process. Agents&nbsp;assume much of&nbsp;the cognitive load&nbsp;associated with&nbsp;sorting, clustering, tracing, and&nbsp;drafting,&nbsp;allowing experts&nbsp;to&nbsp;focus on judgment, prioritization, and implementation.&nbsp;Teams remain accountable for what is fixed, what is funded, and what is allowed to ship.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>

<h3><span><span>Key learnings and transferable practices</span></span><span>&nbsp;</span></h3>
<p><span>The GEM experience offers several lessons for teams building agentic systems around complex, qualitative business processes:</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<ol>
<li><strong><span>Start with&nbsp;real&nbsp;problems, not AI</span></strong><span> - </span>Value comes from understanding the genuine business needs and applying AI where it is demonstrably better than existing approaches.&nbsp;&nbsp;Applying AI without a clearly defined problem often adds complexity without delivering meaningful value.&nbsp;<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);"> </span></li>
<li><strong>Design for the end-to-end workflow</strong><span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);"> -</span><span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;Value comes from connecting&nbsp;insights to the broader business process,&nbsp;including grounding&nbsp;in prior knowledge,&nbsp;prioritization, engineering action, post-fix&nbsp;measurement&nbsp;and reporting.&nbsp;Standalone&nbsp;AI output creates limited value, while an integrated workflow drives outcomes.</span><span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
<li><strong><span>Design for human judgment and accountability</span></strong><span> - </span>Agents can reduce toil and cognitive load, but researchers, product managers, engineers, and leaders&nbsp;remain&nbsp;responsible for&nbsp;validating&nbsp;insights and determining&nbsp;appropriate actions.<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
<li><strong><span>Ground agents in the knowledge of the teams they serve</span></strong><span> - </span>Shared models&nbsp;require&nbsp;local context. Editable configuration files allow teams to define ownership,&nbsp;business&nbsp;priorities,&nbsp;releases,&nbsp;examples, and trusted sources without&nbsp;modifying&nbsp;underlying agents.<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
<li><strong><span>Build observability and feedback mechanisms into the agentic system itself</span></strong><span> - </span>Making analysis inspectable through&nbsp;reasoning traces, source context, and recommendations enables experts to&nbsp;identify&nbsp;gaps, improve outputs, and build trust over time.&nbsp;Build feedback mechanisms directly into the flow of work, making it effortless for users to provide input on the system.<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
<li><strong><span>Tailor outputs to the people making decisions</span></strong><span> - </span>Executives need trends and investment signals. Researchers need evidence and themes. Engineers need reproducible, actionable work.&nbsp;Effective systems deliver the right information to the right audience.<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
<li><strong><span>Start small and iterate quickly</span></strong><span> - </span>The AI&nbsp;landscape continues to evolve rapidly. Begin with a well-defined problem, measure outcomes, learn from feedback, and iterate as capabilities mature.<span style="color: rgb(30, 30, 30);">&nbsp;</span></li>
</ol>
