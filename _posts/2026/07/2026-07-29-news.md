<h2>Introduction: The cheap-token trap</h2>
<p>Token prices alone are a poor economic model for agents. The price of reaching a fixed capability has fallen sharply — In a&nbsp;<a class="lia-external-url" href="https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai_index_report_2025.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">2025 Report Stanford's AI Index</a> reported a roughly 280-fold drop in the cost of GPT-3.5-level inference between late 2022 and late 2024, and Epoch AI tracks steep (if uneven) per-benchmark price declines. The intuitive conclusion is that agents are getting cheaper to run. The operational reality is the opposite.</p>
<p>Agents turn cheaper inference into <strong>longer, stochastic trajectories</strong>: growing context windows, repeated tool schemas, retries, reflection loops, and sub-agent fan-out. In one study of agentic coding, repeated runs of the&nbsp;<em>same</em>&nbsp;agent on the&nbsp;<em>same</em>&nbsp;task varied in token cost by as much as&nbsp;<strong>30×&nbsp;</strong>for coding agents. When a single logical task can cost you thirty times more depending on the path the agent takes, optimizing&nbsp;<em>average cost per token</em> will happily make the wrong system look efficient. Similar argument can be made for other agentic systems where we may need more than one tries, more than one MCP Calls, Reasoning or use of multiple skills, hooks or tool calls to arrive at a completed task.</p>
<p>So, the leading question of token economics isn't "what's the token price?" It's <strong>"what does it cost to get one accepted unit of useful work — and how confident can we be in that number before the agent runs?"</strong></p>
<h2>The unit that actually matters:&nbsp; Cost per accepted task</h2>
<p>I use&nbsp;<strong>token economics</strong>&nbsp;to mean managing the unit economics of useful AI work under uncertainty. The meaningful unit is&nbsp;<strong>cost per accepted task</strong>, not cost per token.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Let A = 1 mean a task passed its acceptance rubric. The long-run unit cost of a policy π is approximately:</p>
<img /></blockquote>
<p>The numerator is expected task cost; the denominator is the probability the output is actually acceptable. This follows the FinOps distinction between successful and unsuccessful AI outputs and the recommendation to connect cost with workload value. It is a working definition for this project, not a quoted standard — but it reframes the engineering problem immediately. A "cheaper" policy that halves cost while dropping acceptance from 95% to 70% is <strong>more expensive per accepted task</strong>, and only this ratio makes that visible.</p>
<p>That reframing turns "pick the cheapest model" into a five-step discipline:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Forecast</strong>&nbsp;a distribution, not a single token estimate.</li>
<li><strong>Select</strong>&nbsp;a cost policy that is plausible for the task and its risk.</li>
<li><strong>Enforce</strong>&nbsp;routing, context, cache, and budget controls during execution.</li>
<li><strong>Evaluate</strong>&nbsp;whether the output still clears a workload-specific quality floor.</li>
<li><strong>Revert</strong> unsafe savings, reconcile predicted vs. actual usage, and calibrate the next forecast.</li>
</ol>
<img />

<p>Here π is the policy; C_task is total task cost; Q_s is quality for a supported segment s with floor Q_min; B is the budget; and ε is the tolerated breach probability. The pieces are all borrowed — stochastic optimization for the expected-cost objective; FrugalGPT and Confident Adaptive Language Modeling for the LLM precedent of cutting cost while preserving performance; SRE service-level objectives for treating "acceptable service" as an action-driving threshold and Group DRO for the insight that <em>averages hide group failures; and</em>&nbsp;Charnes–Cooper chance-constrained programming for the probabilistic budget limit. <strong>The synthesis — wiring them into one agent controller — is the contribution</strong>.</p>
<p>Two honest caveats travel with this controller:&nbsp;Q_s&nbsp;needs a confidence-adjusted lower bound (sparse segments shouldn't trigger changes on two samples), and the chance constraint is&nbsp;<strong>not a guarantee</strong> until your forecast's percentile coverage is calibrated against real traces. A modeled P95 is a planning estimate, not a promised 5% breach bound.</p>
<h2>Two halves of the loop: feed-forward and feedback</h2>
<p>The current work is result of two self-prototypes — <strong>FutureTokenPredictor</strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong>TokenGov</strong> — built to make agent unit economics operable on Azure. These are reusable implementation patterns and experiments.</p>
<p>The controller splits cleanly into a planning half and a runtime half.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>FutureTokenPredictor is the feed-forward side.</strong>&nbsp;It models workflow archetypes and uncertain iteration counts to produce P50/P95-style planning estimates&nbsp;<em>before execution and</em> recommends a policy. It stays&nbsp;<strong>outside the request path</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>TokenGov is the feedback side.</strong> Its request path applies the admitted cost policy; an out-of-band control plane evaluates outcomes and changes externalized policy when quality regresses. Runtime telemetry then flows back to the predictor as calibration data for the next forecast.</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither half is sufficient alone. Prediction without control is a spreadsheet. Control without quality feedback silently degrades your hardest segments. The value is the&nbsp;<strong>wire between them</strong>: a forecast that becomes an enforceable policy, an eval verdict that can reverse a cost action, and actuals that sharpen the next forecast.</p>
<h2>How the equation lands on Azure</h2>
<p>This is where token economics stops being a metric and becomes architecture. Each term in the controller maps to a concrete Azure control:</p>

<p>Most of these primitives already exist and are individually documented: APIM provides token quotas, semantic caching, and token metrics; Foundry Model Router offers cost/balanced/quality routing modes; Foundry cloud evaluation scores datasets and sampled traces. The interesting gap they&nbsp;<em>don't</em>&nbsp;close on their own is the connected mechanism — an evaluation verdict that can constrain or reverse a cost-saving action, and actual usage that improves the next forecast.</p>
<p>Here is the full two-plane view. FutureTokenPredictor forecasts and recommends&nbsp;<em>before</em> execution; TokenGov owns runtime enforcement and quality-triggered reversion; prediction IDs join forecasts to actual telemetry so calibration can improve the next estimate.</p>

<h2>From Concept to Implementation&nbsp;</h2>
<p><strong>Version 1</strong> release the <strong>Token Prediction and forecast ability</strong> using a local mcp server called FutureTokenPredictor using a local MCP server modeled behind a simple UI, where you can create an assessment for your UI Workload. It lets you simple describe the AI / Agentic Solution you want to build and suggested a topology for it. From there , depending on your model selection, the studio, helps you predict the range of token usage and its estimated costs.</p>
<p>In full version, this forecast is used to build a policy and govern your AI Spend accordingly.</p>
