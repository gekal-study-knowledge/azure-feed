<p><em>How to design, build, and grow an agentic operations practice — and what becomes possible once you do.</em></p>
<blockquote>
<p>A note on scope: the patterns in this guide apply to any agentic operations platform. The specifics — the pricing model, the built-in capabilities, the primitives named throughout — are <strong>Azure SRE Agent</strong>. Where something is a property of the product rather than a universal truth, it’s called out.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr />
<h2>Remember when?</h2>
<p>Remember the 3am page? The one where you sat on the edge of the bed with a laptop balanced on your knees, hunting through six dashboards to work out whether the thing that woke you was even real. Half the time it wasn’t.</p>
<p>Remember the cost review? Somebody exports a month of billing to a spreadsheet, three engineers spend a fortnight arguing about which resources are actually orphaned, and by the time you’ve agreed on a plan the next month’s bill has already landed.</p>
<p>Remember the zero-day? The all-hands marathon. Two days of people cancelling everything, tracing which services pulled the affected package, hand-patching in an order nobody had time to write down.</p>
<p>And remember the CVE backlog — the one everyone knows about, the one that only ever grows, because triaging it properly would take a team you don’t have?</p>
<p><strong>None of that was a failure of effort.</strong> It was the operating model. For decades it looked like this: <em>humans operated, software assisted.</em> We built dashboards, alerts, runbooks, automation scripts, and eventually copilots — and through every one of those advances, the human was still the operator.</p>
<p>That’s the part that’s changing. And it’s genuinely good news.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Agents operate. Humans govern.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>That’s Zero Ops. And the best part is you don’t have to invent it — the path is already well-worn.</p>
<hr />
<h2>The five things worth knowing before you start</h2>
<p>Everything below comes from building and running agentic operations at scale. If you read nothing else, read these.</p>
<p><strong>1. Zero Ops is the destination — and it doesn’t mean zero humans.</strong> It means <em>removing operations from humans</em>. People don’t disappear; they move up the stack. They set the intent, govern the system, and validate outcomes. Nobody’s job becomes “watch the dashboard” ever again.</p>
<p><strong>2. The model is not the moat.</strong> This was the biggest surprise. The model matters less every year. You can swap models. What you cannot swap is the <strong>context and governance</strong> wrapped around them. That’s the durable asset you’re building.</p>
<p><strong>3. Context creates intelligence.</strong> Agents become genuinely useful the moment they’re grounded in reality — your source code, your live telemetry, your institutional knowledge, your incident history, and the skills and tools to act on all of it. Swap the model and the system still works. Swap the context and it stops being useful.</p>
<p><strong>4. Governance creates trust.</strong> Enterprises don’t trust intelligence. Enterprises trust controls. Identity, audit, evals, rollback, evidence. Governance is what <em>earns the right</em> to automate — and it’s liberating rather than restricting, because it’s what lets you say yes.</p>
<p><strong>5. Metrics create permission.</strong> Nobody should trust an agent because a demo looked impressive. Trust comes from numbers you can run yourself. If only the vendor can produce the number, it’s marketing. If you can query it, it’s a metric.</p>
<h3>The climb, and the one thing that changes at each rung</h3>
<p>Here’s the elegant part. As an agent matures, the thing that changes isn’t how clever it is. It’s <strong>what the human reviews.</strong></p>
<div class="styles_lia-table-wrapper__h6Xo9 styles_table-responsive__MW0lN"><table><thead><tr><th>Rung</th><th>What the agent does</th><th>What the human reviews</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Crawl</strong></td><td>Suggests. A human still does the work.</td><td>Their own work</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Walk</strong></td><td>Does the work one step at a time, asking before each action.</td><td>Every step</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Run</strong></td><td>Completes whole tasks and hands back a change to approve.</td><td>The diff</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Fly</strong></td><td>Fixes, deploys to test, validates the outcome itself, posts the evidence.</td><td>The outcome</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>And between Run and Fly sits <strong>the review wall</strong>. When an agent produces hundreds of changes a month, reviewing someone else’s diff is nearly as hard as writing it yourself. That’s where teams plateau — not because the agent isn’t capable, but because the humans became the bottleneck.</p>
<p>Fly is how you get past it: you move the unit of human review from <em>the diff</em> to <em>the outcome</em>. Hold that thought — we’ll come back to it, because it’s the most exciting part of the whole journey.</p>
<p>Getting there is a design problem before it’s a technology one. Agents that climb were built to climb. So let’s start where every one of them starts — how you scope it, what you teach it, and what you connect it to.</p>
<hr />
<h1>Part One — Designing your agent</h1>
<h2>Before you start: what you’ll want in place</h2>
<p>The good news is that this list is short, and you almost certainly have most of it already. There’s no platform to stand up first.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Diagnostic logs turned on</strong> for the services you care about. An agent can only reason about what your system actually emits.</li>
<li><strong>Telemetry the agent can query.</strong> It doesn’t need to live in one place — most estates have it spread across several platforms, and that’s completely fine. What matters is that each of those places is reachable and queryable. This is what turns “something is wrong” into “here’s why.”</li>
<li><strong>Read access to the sources that hold the answers</strong> — your subscriptions, your repositories, your incident history, your ticketing system.</li>
<li><strong>An identity for the agent</strong>, with permissions scoped the way you’d scope a new team member’s on day one.</li>
<li><strong>A repository for agent artifacts.</strong> Skills, custom agents and tool definitions are production code. They deserve version control from the first one.</li>
</ul>
<p>That’s it. Nothing here is agent-specific — it’s the same hygiene that makes a system operable by humans. If your on-call engineer can answer a question at 3am, your agent can too.</p>
<h2>Step 1: Scope it — how many agents do you actually need?</h2>
<p>Good news first: fewer than you think. Teams often assume one agent per team, and that’s usually wrong. Five considerations decide it:</p>
<p><strong>1. Fixed cost.</strong> Every Azure SRE Agent carries a small baseline charge just for existing — think of it as keeping the lights on so the agent is ready the instant something happens. That means consolidating where you can is genuinely good hygiene: fewer agents, each with a clear job, means every dollar goes toward outcomes rather than idle capacity.</p>
<p><strong>2. Context.</strong> This is the big one. An agent is powerful <em>because</em> it holds a complete picture of a system. Split one application’s context across two agents and you’ve halved what each of them knows — usually the half that mattered. <strong>Don’t split an app’s context.</strong></p>
<p><strong>3. Data residency at rest.</strong> If data legally cannot leave a geography, that’s a boundary, and it’s a real one. Separate agent, separate region.</p>
<p><strong>4. Team and organisational access boundaries.</strong> Genuinely different permission sets and genuinely different blast radius deserve genuinely different agents — each with its own identity, so least-privilege actually means something.</p>
<p><strong>5. At least one dev agent.</strong> Always keep a non-production agent to test changes before they touch prod. Same reason you have a staging environment.</p>
<p>That’s the whole list. Everything else, consolidate.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Ideally, this is what it looks like.</strong> A single agent per application or product module — never splitting one across two. Explicit production and test agents. A regional agent wherever residency genuinely demands one. Every split maps to one of the five considerations above.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The one thing to protect in every split decision is <strong>context</strong>. When two agents need to reason about the same problem, each one only has half the picture. If you absolutely must split context — say your org structure or access boundaries require it — plan for those agents to talk to each other so the full context is still reachable.</p>
<p>Multiple patterns work. A dedicated infrastructure team that manages AKS clusters and only cares about the upkeep of that infrastructure? A single agent scoped to those resources makes perfect sense — they have a clear domain, a clear boundary, and a clear job. An application team whose service depends on a database? Give <em>that application’s</em> agent access to the database rather than standing up a second agent and splitting the problem’s context across two. There’s no single right layout — the principle is: <strong>keep the context of the problems you’re trying to solve together.</strong></p>
<h2>Step 2: Teach it — context is king</h2>
<p>This is where the magic actually comes from, and it’s the step most worth over-investing in.</p>
<p>Your agent needs five kinds of context:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source code</strong> — what the system actually does</li>
<li><strong>Production telemetry</strong> — what it’s doing right now</li>
<li><strong>Institutional knowledge</strong> — how your team really operates</li>
<li><strong>Previous incidents</strong> — what broke before, and why</li>
<li><strong>Skills and tools</strong> — how to act on any of it</li>
</ul>
<p>Connect the first two and you have a competent log reader. Add the middle two and it starts sounding like someone who’s worked on your team for a year.</p>
<p><strong>How you actually bring context in:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Connect the real sources</strong> — subscriptions and their telemetry, your repositories, your incident history, your ticketing system.</li>
<li><strong>Knowledge as markdown in a repo.</strong> This is the pattern that works best. LLMs are exceptionally good with markdown files, and putting your knowledge in a connected repository means it’s version-controlled, reviewable, and — critically — updatable by the agent itself. Your scheduled tasks can automatically improve these files as the agent learns, closing the loop between insight and artifact.</li>
<li><strong>Connect external knowledge via MCP.</strong> If your team’s knowledge lives in Confluence, SharePoint, or another platform, connect it as an MCP server rather than migrating it. The agent queries it at runtime.</li>
<li><strong>Upload documents.</strong> Architecture diagrams, architecture decision records, design docs, onboarding guides. It reads all of it.</li>
<li><strong>Just talk to it.</strong> This is the underrated one. Tell it how your system works. Explain that “the blue cluster” means the EU stamp, that Tuesday deploys are riskier, that this alert is always noise before 8am. Ask it to summarise your architecture back to you — where it’s wrong, you’ve found a context gap, and you can fill it on the spot.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>One thing to be deliberate about: don’t dump everything.</strong> If you’ve accumulated years of documentation, runbooks, and tribal knowledge, resist the urge to pour all of it in on day one. Garbage in, garbage out. The agent will work with whatever you give it, and outdated or contradictory knowledge makes it worse, not better. <strong>Curate intentionally.</strong> Start with the knowledge that matters for the scenarios you’re tackling first, make sure it’s current, and grow from there.</p>
<p>Teaching an agent feels remarkably like onboarding a sharp new hire. The difference is it reads everything you give it, overnight, and never forgets.</p>
<p><strong>And you don’t have to teach it everything at once.</strong></p>
<p>This is the part worth saying plainly, because the size of an estate can feel paralysing. You are not trying to pour your entire organisation into an agent before it becomes useful. You teach it the parts that matter, and you do it organically — <strong>one solution at a time</strong>.</p>
<p>Start from your toil. Write down the things that actually wake your engineers up, the tasks your team does over and over, the investigation everyone dreads because it takes four hours and always ends the same way. Pick the top one. Coach the agent through that single scenario the way you’d coach a new engineer through their first on-call shift — the context it needs, the sources it should check, the judgement calls that aren’t written down anywhere.</p>
<p>Then do the next one.</p>
<p>Each scenario you teach is narrow, which means it’s cheap and fast to get right. And each one compounds: the context you gave it for scenario one is already there when you start scenario three. Six weeks in, you’ll notice it knows your system well enough to help with things you never explicitly taught it.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Don’t boil the ocean. Boil the thing that’s burning you.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Step 3: Create the artifacts — understand the primitives, then build</h2>
<p>Before you build anything, it helps to understand the three primitives you’re building with — because the difference between them is what gives you consistency.</p>
<p><strong>The meta agent</strong> is your agent out of the box. It has the LLM’s world knowledge plus all the context you’ve connected — your code, your telemetry, your documents, your memory. It’s versatile: it can investigate, reason, plan, and act. But it’s non-deterministic. Ask it the same question twice and it might take different steps, in a different order, and format its findings differently. That’s fine for exploration. It’s not fine for the 3am incident that needs to run the same way every time.</p>
<p><strong>Custom agents</strong> give you that consistency. A custom agent is a specialist with its own instructions, its own tools, and its own scope. Think of it as the <em>what</em> — the plan. The <a href="https://github.com/microsoft/sre-agent/tree/main/labs/zava-learning" target="_blank">Zava learning lab</a>’s learning-ops agent is a good example: it tells the agent exactly how to handle an incident — what to check, in what order, what to post, how to format the report. Every run follows that plan. Custom agents are scoped — they’re only invoked when you specifically ask for them (via <code>/agent</code> in chat, or via a response plan or scheduled task). That scoping is itself a governance lever, which we’ll come back to in Step 5.</p>
<p><strong>Skills</strong> are the <em>how</em>. They’re reusable procedures that teach the agent how to do a specific thing — query your Kusto cluster, restart a container app, read an IcM incident, run a particular diagnostic sequence. Skills are universal: both the meta agent and any custom agent can use them. A single skill written once is available everywhere.</p>
<p>The key insight: <strong>the meta agent alone will get you far, but it won’t do the same ten steps next time, or in the same order, or produce the same kind of report.</strong> Custom agents and skills give you that repeatability — and repeatability is what you need for automation you trust.</p>
<p>Now — how you actually create them. There are exactly two on-ramps, and which one you take depends on whether you already know the answer.</p>
<p><strong>Path A — you have a runbook (a known problem).</strong> Throw the runbook at the agent and ask it to build the artifacts: the skill, the custom agent, the tool definitions. Review what it produces, refine it, and have it cut a pull request into your repository. A procedure you’d have hand-written over a week arrives in an afternoon.</p>
<p><strong>Path B — you don’t (a complex or unknown problem).</strong> Work it interactively. Hand the agent the live problem and investigate <em>together</em>. Let it dig, watch it waver, correct its wrong turns, point it at the source it didn’t know about. When you finally crack it — <strong>that’s</strong> the moment. Ask it to turn what just happened into a custom agent, a skill, a tool.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>The next time that problem appears, it’s automatic.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Path B is the one people don’t expect, and it’s the more valuable of the two. Your best artifacts aren’t written at a desk. They’re <strong>precipitated out of real investigations that worked.</strong> Every hard incident you solve together becomes an incident you never have to solve again.</p>
<p>This isn’t unusual, either — teams everywhere now run skill-creating skills, agent-building skills, and MCP-server-building skills. Using the agent to build more of the agent is simply how this works now.</p>
<h2>Step 4: Test it — playground first, then non-prod</h2>
<p>Treat agent artifacts like code, because they are.</p>
<p>Start in the <strong>playground</strong> — a safe space to exercise a skill against realistic inputs without touching anything. Then promote to a <strong>non-production system</strong> where the agent can act for real against resources that don’t matter.</p>
<p>You won’t get everything right before production, and you don’t need to. Get the <strong>critical parts</strong> right — the core logic, the safety boundaries, the happy path — and then put it on real work. That’s where you find out what it’s actually like.</p>
<p>From there, use <strong>evals</strong> to improve continuously. Every real run produces one, and reading them is how you find out whether the artifact holds up outside the playground. Part Two covers what to do with that signal — including how to wire it back into the artifacts automatically.</p>
<p>And because these are production artifacts, they belong in <strong>source control</strong> from the beginning — with review, diffs, and rollback.</p>
<h2>Step 5: Govern it — earn the right to automate</h2>
<p>Remember principle four: <em>governance creates trust</em>. This is where you make it concrete. Before anything touches production, you decide <strong>who the agent is</strong>, <strong>what it’s allowed to do</strong>, <strong>what rules gate its actions</strong>, and <strong>what checks run in context</strong>. These controls layer on top of each other, and together they’re what lets you say yes to autonomy with confidence.</p>
<h3>Identity and access</h3>
<p>Your agent authenticates as a <strong>managed identity</strong> — system-assigned or user-assigned — and you scope it with normal Azure RBAC at the <strong>subscription</strong>, <strong>resource group</strong>, or <strong>management group</strong> level.</p>
<p>Out of the box, Azure SRE Agent offers two access tiers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reader</strong> — read-only access to your resources. This is all your agent needs for investigation, root-cause analysis, and reporting. It’s the right starting point.</li>
<li><strong>Privileged</strong> — adds resource-type-specific contributor roles (like Container App Contributor) based on what’s detected in your environment. This is what the agent needs for actions: restart, scale, rollback, configuration changes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Most teams start with Reader and add Privileged only on the resource groups where they want the agent to act. If neither tier fits — maybe you want the agent to restart App Services but never touch network rules — create a <strong>custom RBAC role</strong> with exactly the permissions you need and assign it to the agent’s managed identity. The agent’s identity is its security boundary; treat it the way you’d treat any other service principal.</p>
<h3>Run mode</h3>
<p>This is the single biggest lever. In <strong>Review mode</strong>, the agent proposes actions and waits for a human to approve each one. In <strong>Autonomous mode</strong>, it acts on its own within the bounds you’ve set. Most teams start every scenario in Review, watch it work for a few weeks, and then selectively move well-understood scenarios to Autonomous. That graduation <em>is</em> the Crawl-to-Run climb in practice.</p>
<p>There’s a third thing worth understanding: <strong>what happens when the agent doesn’t have the privileges to act.</strong> If the agent’s managed identity lacks the RBAC permission for an action, it doesn’t fail silently — it asks. An Administrator can grant <strong>temporary elevation via on-behalf-of (OBO)</strong>, which lets the action execute using the human’s credentials rather than the agent’s identity. This is the human-in-the-loop pattern at its most precise: the agent does the investigation, proposes the action, and a human with the right privileges authorises it in context. The agent never accumulates permissions it doesn’t need permanently, and the audit trail shows exactly who approved what.</p>
<h3>Tool controls</h3>
<p>Every tool the agent has access to can be set to one of three states:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Allow</strong> — the tool executes without asking. Good for safe read operations you’re confident about.</li>
<li><strong>Ask</strong> — the tool pauses for human approval before running. Good for actions you trust but want to see before they happen.</li>
<li><strong>Off</strong> — the tool is completely disabled. The agent can’t use it at all.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is the first governance layer — simple, per-tool toggles. Need the agent to query your Kusto cluster but never write to it? Allow the read tool, turn the write tool off. Need it to restart an App Service but never delete one? Allow the restart, turn delete off. This is how you define the agent’s basic operational envelope.</p>
<p>Some actions — like restarting a healthy service or scaling up a container app — may not need any gating at all. Others — like modifying a network security group or changing a database configuration — absolutely do. The right setting depends on how much autonomy you want the agent to have and how much you want a human involved. There’s no single right answer; there’s the answer that fits your comfort level today, and it can change tomorrow.</p>
<h3>Tool access policies</h3>
<p>Tool controls are per-tool on/off switches. Tool access policies go deeper: they let you write <strong>pattern-based rules</strong> that match tool names and even command arguments.</p>
<p>Examples: - <em>“Deny any command containing <code>delete</code>“</em> — <code>bash(az * delete *)</code> matches any <code>az ... delete ...</code> command regardless of which tool executes it. - <em>“Allow all monitoring queries without approval”</em> — so your read-only investigation flow runs uninterrupted. - <em>“Ask before any deployment command”</em> — so deploys always pause for a human.</p>
<p>Policies apply at three scopes:</p>
<div class="styles_lia-table-wrapper__h6Xo9 styles_table-responsive__MW0lN"><table><thead><tr><th>Scope</th><th>Who sets it</th><th>What it can do</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Global</strong></td><td>Admin</td><td>Allow, Ask, or <strong>Deny</strong> — across the entire agent</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Custom agent</strong></td><td>Admin or author</td><td>Allow only — widen access within global boundaries for a specific custom agent</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Thread</strong></td><td>Any user</td><td>Allow only — temporary override for one conversation</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>The key principle: a global deny cannot be overridden by a lower scope. A custom agent or thread can <em>widen</em> access but never weaken a global deny. This means an admin can set a floor — “nobody, human or agent, can run a delete command” — and know it holds.</p>
<h3>Hooks</h3>
<p>Policies match patterns. Hooks evaluate <strong>context</strong>. This is the layer that handles the cases patterns can’t express.</p>
<p>Four hook events:</p>
<div class="styles_lia-table-wrapper__h6Xo9 styles_table-responsive__MW0lN"><table><thead><tr><th>Event</th><th>When it fires</th><th>What you’d use it for</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Start</strong></td><td>A new thread begins</td><td>Seed context, validate the trigger, tag the conversation</td></tr><tr><td><strong>PreToolUse</strong></td><td>The agent is about to call a tool</td><td>Inspect the arguments, allow/deny/ask based on what you see</td></tr><tr><td><strong>PostToolUse</strong></td><td>A tool just returned</td><td>Audit the result, flag sensitive output, trigger follow-up</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Stop</strong></td><td>The agent is about to finish</td><td>Validate that the work is complete, reject and keep the loop running if it’s not</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Hooks can be <strong>prompt-based</strong> (an LLM judge evaluates the situation) or <strong>command-based</strong> (a bash or Python script runs deterministically). They sit at the <strong>highest priority</strong> in the decision chain — a hook allow overrides everything below it, and a hook deny blocks immediately.</p>
<p>Here’s where it gets practical. Say the agent is investigating a performance issue and discovers a corrupt database index. It decides to drop and rebuild the index — exactly what a DBA would do. But you don’t want the agent to ever drop a <em>table</em>. How do you allow one and prevent the other?</p>
<p>Three layers, working together:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Tool access policy</strong>: a global deny on any command matching <code>*DROP TABLE*</code>. Pattern-based, unconditional, always enforced.</li>
<li><strong>Custom agent scoping</strong>: create a <em>database-maintenance</em> custom agent with instructions that explicitly say “you may drop and rebuild indexes; you may never drop tables.” The custom agent only has the database tools it needs — nothing else. The blast radius is contained by design.</li>
<li><strong>PreToolUse hook</strong>: a script that inspects the actual SQL command. It allows <code>DROP INDEX</code>, denies <code>DROP TABLE</code>, and can require approval for any DDL command above a risk threshold you define.</li>
</ol>
<p>The policy catches the obvious pattern. The custom agent constrains the scope. The hook handles the edge cases that patterns miss. This is the full stack working together.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Scoping automations to a custom agent is one of the most powerful governance levers you have.</strong> Instead of giving the meta agent broad database access, you create a specialist with its own tools, its own instructions, its own tool access policies, and its own hooks. The meta agent can investigate and recommend. Actual database changes only happen through the custom agent, with guardrails purpose-built for that domain.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3>Who can configure the agent</h3>
<p>RBAC extends to the agent itself. Four built-in roles govern who can do what:</p>
<div class="styles_lia-table-wrapper__h6Xo9 styles_table-responsive__MW0lN"><table><thead><tr><th>Role</th><th>What they can do</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Administrator</strong></td><td>Full control — approve actions, manage connectors, configure hooks and policies, change run mode, deploy artifacts</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Author</strong></td><td>Create custom agents and tools, upload knowledge, author response plans and incident configurations, manage connectors</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Standard User</strong></td><td>Chat, run diagnostics, request actions, create scheduled tasks</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Reader</strong></td><td>View conversations and configuration — read-only</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Separation of duties applies here the same way it applies everywhere else: the person who builds a skill shouldn’t necessarily be the person who promotes it to Autonomous. Only Administrators can approve infrastructure actions — Standard Users and Authors cannot. And only Administrators can create hooks and tool access policies, because those controls govern what every other role can do.</p>
<h3>How it all fits together</h3>
<p>These controls layer: identity sets the <em>boundary</em>, run mode sets the <em>default posture</em>, tool controls set the <em>envelope</em>, policies set the <em>rules</em>, and hooks handle the <em>judgement calls</em>. They’re not restrictions — they’re what lets you say yes to progressively more autonomy, with evidence that each step is safe.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>A useful mental model:</strong> governance isn’t a gate you pass through once. It’s the dial you turn up gradually, scenario by scenario, as each one proves itself. The agent that’s fully autonomous for certificate renewals and fully gated for database changes isn’t half-governed — it’s precisely governed.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Step 6: Promote to production — your agent configuration is code</h2>
<p>This is the step that turns your dev agent into a repeatable, auditable production system.</p>
<p>Your dev agent is your workshop — the place where you experiment, teach, build artifacts, and iterate until things work. Once they do, the configuration you’ve built there becomes your <strong>golden state</strong>: the skills, custom agents, tool definitions, knowledge base, response plans, scheduled tasks, and memory that together define how this agent operates.</p>
<p>All of it can be declared, versioned, and deployed programmatically:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Infrastructure as Code</strong> — define agents and their configuration in Bicep or Terraform, same as any other Azure resource. Your agent’s entire shape lives in a template.</li>
<li><strong>CLI and REST API</strong> — create, update, and configure agents with <code>az</code> commands or direct API calls. Useful for CI/CD pipelines that promote artifacts from dev to prod as part of a normal release.</li>
<li><strong>Artifact repositories</strong> — skills, custom agents, and tool definitions are files in your repo. Push them to your production agent the same way you push code: through a pipeline, with review, with rollback.</li>
</ul>
<p>This means everything your dev agent learned can flow to production through your existing change process. A new skill gets built and tested in dev, reviewed in a PR, merged, and deployed to the production agent by the pipeline — no portal clicking, no manual replication, no drift.</p>
<p>It also means <strong>consistency across a fleet</strong>. If you run multiple agents — per module, per region, per environment — they can all be powered from the same artifact repository. Update the skill once, deploy it everywhere. The agent-per-module pattern from Step 1 works precisely because IaC makes it cheap to keep them consistent.</p>
<p>And when something goes wrong, you roll back the same way you roll back anything else: revert the commit, redeploy the template, and the agent is back to its last known-good state.</p>
<h2>Step 7: Wire it up — an artifact does nothing until something calls it</h2>
<p>A skill sitting in your repository doesn’t do anything until it’s <strong>bound to a trigger</strong> — something in your world that fires it without a human deciding to. It’s an easy step to skip, and worth not skipping.</p>
<p>There are three you’ll use constantly:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Incident response plans.</strong> Attach the artifact to an alert class, so that when <em>that</em> alert fires, <em>that</em> skill runs. This is the single highest-value wiring you can do.</li>
<li><strong>Scheduled tasks.</strong> For work that should happen on a rhythm rather than in response to a signal — the nightly sweep, the weekly review, the monthly audit.</li>
<li><strong>HTTP triggers.</strong> For everything else in your ecosystem that wants to start agent work: a pipeline stage, a webhook, a work item transitioning to Ready.</li>
</ul>
<p>Here’s why this matters more than it looks. Remember the climb — Crawl, Walk, Run, Fly. Teams often assume they’ll graduate by making the agent <em>smarter</em>. They won’t. A brilliantly capable agent that only ever runs when someone opens a chat window is still at Walk, permanently, because a human is still initiating every piece of work.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Capability doesn’t promote you. Triggers do.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Wiring is the actual line between Walk and Run. Cross it deliberately.</p>
<h2>Step 8: Mimic your production process</h2>
<p>Here’s the step that turns a clever assistant into an operations teammate: <strong>the agent should follow the process your humans already follow.</strong></p>
<p>Not a parallel workflow. <em>The</em> workflow.</p>
<p>An incident arrives → the agent <strong>acknowledges</strong> it, so everyone can see it’s owned → it <strong>investigates</strong>, pulling telemetry, correlating recent deploys, checking dependencies → it <strong>posts its findings</strong> to the incident, where the on-call already lives → it <strong>proposes or applies</strong> the mitigation → it <strong>updates status</strong> → it <strong>documents the root cause</strong> → it <strong>resolves</strong>.</p>
<p>That’s end-to-end incident investigation and remediation, in the same lifecycle, the same ticket, the same channel your team already watches. No new tool to learn. The on-call engineer just notices the work is already done.</p>
<p>And this covers more ground than people expect. Most teams think of incidents in three flavours: <strong>outages</strong>, where something is down; <strong>performance issues</strong>, where something is slow or degrading; and <strong>manual errors</strong> — the config change somebody made by hand at the end of a long day, the setting that got flipped in the portal and never made it back into source control. That third category is the one teams under-count and the one agents are unusually good at, because catching it is mostly a matter of comparing what’s running against what was declared — patient, unglamorous work that nobody wants to do at 2am.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>A worked example.</strong> The <a href="https://github.com/microsoft/sre-agent/tree/main/labs/zava-learning" target="_blank">Zava learning lab</a> agent runs exactly this loop — incident-triggered investigation and remediation across a live estate. Looking at its real runs: a typical end-to-end run takes about <strong>32 tool calls</strong> and lands in a <strong>5-to-12-minute</strong> band, with a median around <strong>8.6 minutes</strong> from signal to finished work. Roughly five minutes to a mitigation, ten to a full resolution.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Compare that with what it replaces: a page, someone waking up, ten minutes to orient, a scramble across dashboards, a colleague pulled in for a second opinion. Ninety minutes and two engineers, on a good night.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>A second example, from the other end of the lifecycle.</strong> A large software vendor running a multi-region estate — dozens of subscriptions, tens of thousands of resources — wired their agents into delivery rather than just incidents. A work item moves to Ready, and that’s the trigger. A custom agent picks it up, writes the code, opens the pull request, deploys the change to a test environment, and runs the validation suite against it — synthetic checks and browser-driven tests, the same ones a human would have run. Then it posts the result on the original work item.</p>
<p>The engineer’s first involvement is reading an outcome that already has evidence attached. Same five design considerations from Step 1 govern their fleet: one agent per product module, explicit prod and test agents, and one extra agent in-region purely for data residency.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>These two examples bookend the same idea. One agent closes incidents; the other closes work items. Both were built the same way — context first, artifacts second, triggers third.</p>
<h3>Beyond incidents — the agent as a proactive partner</h3>
<p>It’s easy to think of agents as incident responders, because that’s where the value is most visible. But the best teams use them just as heavily when nothing is broken.</p>
<p><strong>Understand your system better.</strong> Ask the agent to explain your architecture back to you. Ask it what depends on what. Ask it to map the blast radius of a change you haven’t made yet. Ask it to find every resource in your estate that hasn’t been touched in six months, or every configuration that drifts from what’s declared in code. These aren’t investigations — they’re conversations. And the answers come grounded in your actual telemetry and source code, not a wiki page that was last updated in 2023.</p>
<p><strong>Get recommendations you didn’t ask for.</strong> Set up a scheduled task that reviews your infrastructure weekly and surfaces opportunities: resources that could be right-sized, SKUs that could be downgraded, replicas that could be consolidated, regions where you’re paying for redundancy you’re not using. The same task can check for reliability gaps: services without health probes configured, storage accounts without soft-delete enabled, deployments running without a rollback path. The agent sees all of it because it already has the context — it just needs a reason to look.</p>
<p><strong>Run proactive reliability reviews.</strong> Ask the agent to evaluate your service against the Azure Well-Architected Framework, or against your own best practices checklist. Ask it to compare your production configuration against your staging configuration and tell you what’s different — and whether the difference is intentional. Ask it to trace a customer-facing flow end to end and identify the single points of failure.</p>
<p><strong>Shift from reactive to preventive.</strong> This is the compounding value of the platform. Every incident the agent resolves teaches it something about your system. Over time, the agent that started as an incident responder becomes the thing that <em>prevents</em> incidents — because it’s seen enough of your system to spot the preconditions before they become symptoms. The cost analysis that catches the runaway resource before finance does. The capacity check that raises the quota before the 429s start. The configuration audit that catches the drift before it becomes an outage.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>The agent that only responds to incidents is useful. The agent that also prevents them is transformative.</strong></p>
</blockquote>

<h1>Part Two — Running it well</h1>
<h2>Two loops, not one queue</h2>
<p>Once agents are working real incidents, the question stops being <em>can it</em> and becomes <em>which ones</em>. Make that a routing decision rather than a judgement call.</p>
<p>Run two loops: an <strong>agent loop</strong> and a <strong>human loop</strong>. Every incident class is registered to one of them, so where an incident lands is a property of the class, decided in advance — not something someone works out at 3am.</p>
<p>Promotion between loops is deliberate. Moving a class into the agent loop is a reviewable change with a written gate, and a written demotion trigger for when it stops earning its place.</p>
<p><strong>The safety net belongs to the incident system, not the agent.</strong> Define the conditions your process cares about — not acknowledged within <em>x</em> minutes, not mitigated, not handed off — and let the incident system escalate to the human loop when they’re breached. An agent that has stalled can’t be relied on to report that it has stalled; something outside it has to notice.</p>
<p>And escalation should carry the work with it, so the human arrives to evidence already gathered rather than a blank page.</p>
<h2>Cost: know what an outcome costs</h2>
<p>One of the quietly wonderful things about agentic operations is that <strong>you can finally price an outcome.</strong></p>
<p>Agents consume metered units, and every unit maps to work. So instead of “what does our on-call cost?” — a question nobody has ever answered honestly — you get: <em>this incident, end to end, cost this much.</em></p>
<p>For the loop above: an entire incident investigated, mitigated, documented and resolved, in minutes, for <strong>under $30</strong>.</p>
<p>Now price the alternative. Two engineers, ninety minutes, out of hours, plus the context-switch tax on whatever they were doing, plus the meeting the next morning to explain what happened. You’re comparing tens of dollars against hundreds — and that’s before you count the ninety minutes of customer impact that didn’t happen because the fix landed in eight minutes instead of an hour and a half.</p>
<p>Multiply by your monthly incident volume and it stops being a cost conversation and starts being a capacity one. <strong>The question isn’t “can we afford this?” — it’s “what do we do with the engineering time we just got back?”</strong></p>
<p>But be careful not to measure the return only in money and minutes, because the larger part of it never shows up on an invoice. It’s the engineer who slept through the night. It’s the on-call rotation people stop quietly dreading, and the weekend that stayed a weekend. It’s the postmortem that never had to be written — and with it, the whole uncomfortable ritual of working out whose change it was — because the problem was caught and fixed while it was still one degraded instance rather than a customer-visible outage.</p>
<p>Teams feel that long before finance notices the bill. Morale has always been a reliability metric; it just never had a dashboard.</p>
<p>A few practical habits: - Set a consumption budget deliberately, and know <strong>who can raise it and how fast</strong> before you need them. - Watch cost <em>per resolved outcome</em>, not total spend. Total spend rising while cost-per-outcome falls is exactly what success looks like. - <strong>Use the right trigger for the job.</strong> Incident response plans and HTTP triggers bring the work to the agent the instant it matters. Scheduled tasks handle the work that belongs on a rhythm — the nightly sweep, the weekly review. Both have a place; the key is matching each scenario to the trigger that fits it.</p>
<h2>Live Reports — the UI beyond chat</h2>
<p>Most people first encounter their agent in a chat window, and chat is genuinely good for investigation and conversation. But it’s not the only surface — and for a lot of operational work, it’s not the best one.</p>
<p><strong>Live Reports</strong> are interactive HTML applications built by the agent and hosted on the platform. They call the same tools the agent uses — Kusto queries, Azure CLI, incident APIs, connector tools — and render the results as charts, tables, grids, and interactive controls. They’re not screenshots of a past conversation. They’re <strong>live applications that re-fetch data every time you open them</strong>.</p>
<p>Here’s the part worth understanding from a cost perspective: the agent spends tokens when it <em>builds</em> the report — the conversation where you describe what you want. After that, opening the report calls the tools directly. No LLM is involved, so <strong>there’s no ongoing token consumption</strong>. Build it once, open it a hundred times, share it with your team — the investment is in the creation, and it pays off every time someone opens it.</p>
<p>Think of Live Reports as the place where your agent’s intelligence becomes a <strong>permanent, shareable surface</strong> rather than a conversation that scrolls away.</p>
<p><strong>Scenarios where Live Reports shine:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>
<p><strong>Morning triage view.</strong> What happened overnight? Which incidents are open, which were resolved autonomously, which need human attention? A single page your on-call opens at the start of every shift — always current, no queries to run.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Agent fleet health.</strong> Across all your agents: which are healthy, which have degraded tool reliability, which haven’t run in a week? Per-tool success rates, outcome counts, cost-per-resolution trending. The monitoring dashboard you’d otherwise build in Grafana, except it’s already wired to the data.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Governance and compliance.</strong> NSG audit results, CVE exposure by service, resource compliance against your policy baseline. The report that used to take two engineers a day to compile — now it’s a page that’s always current.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Cost analysis.</strong> Per-agent spend, per-outcome cost, consumption trending with visual charts. The data that makes the cost conversation in Part Two actually work.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>On-call handover.</strong> A shift handoff report: what happened during this rotation, what’s still pending, what to watch. Built once, regenerated for every handover.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Stakeholder status pages.</strong> Service health for leadership or customers — uptime, incident summary, SLA adherence — without exposing the underlying tools or conversations.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Interactive explorers.</strong> Not just viewing data but acting on it. A compliance report where you can drill into a finding and ask the agent to open a remediation PR, right from the report surface.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The pattern is the same every time: you tell the agent what you want to see, it builds the report, and from that point forward the report is a <strong>zero-cost, always-current application</strong> that anyone on your team can open. It’s the agent’s intelligence crystallised into a surface that doesn’t need the agent to be running.</p>

<h2>Knowledge, evals and learning — insist on these</h2>
<p>Context isn’t a one-time setup. It’s a living asset, and it’s the thing that compounds — but only if your platform is built to let it. This is the section where what you’re running starts to matter a great deal, so it’s worth being direct about what to demand.</p>
<p><strong>Insist on an agent that learns without being told to.</strong> The common failure mode of agentic tooling is that all the good material stays in the chat thread. Someone works a hard problem with the agent at midnight, finally cracks it, and the reasoning evaporates when the tab closes. What you want instead is <em>derived learning</em>: the agent distils what it just worked out — the query that got there, the dead end worth avoiding, the service that behaves nothing like its documentation — and files it as durable, structured knowledge on its own, without anyone remembering to write it down. Azure SRE Agent does this automatically. Every investigation deposits something.</p>
<p>What still needs your attention is the <strong>round trip to the original source of truth.</strong> Derived learning lives with the agent. Your runbook, your architecture note, your alert definition lives in your repository — and that’s the copy your humans read. Wire the automation that pushes a learning back into the original artifact as a pull request, so the knowledge doesn’t quietly fork into two versions. This is the single most valuable piece of plumbing most teams haven’t built yet.</p>
<p><strong>Insist on evals that run forever, not once.</strong> Evals get widely misread as a pre-production gate: test the skill, it passes, it ships, done. That’s the smaller half of the value. The bigger half is relentless — continuously evaluating the agent’s <em>real</em> runs in production. Did it stay in scope? Did it reach the right conclusion? Did it stop and ask when it should have? Did that skill quietly start failing at step four last Tuesday? Real traffic finds things no test suite will, and it finds them on your actual estate rather than on a fixture.</p>
<p>Then close the loop, so eval results become work rather than a report nobody opens. Azure SRE Agent ships this as a first-class loop: scheduled tasks that watch the eval signal, notice the degradation, and act on it.</p>
<h2>Self-improvement — where it gets fun</h2>
<p>Which is where something rather lovely happens: <strong>the agent starts improving itself.</strong></p>
<p>It notices a runbook is out of date and updates it. It sees a skill failing at the same step and rewrites that step. It spots a recurring investigation and proposes a new custom agent to own it. It watches its own eval scores and opens a pull request against the artifact that slipped. Teams run learning-loop agents alongside their fleets, and watchdog agents that review other agents’ work.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Every completed task should make the next task easier.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>That’s the flywheel — and it only turns when all three pieces are present: knowledge that accumulates by itself, evals that keep scoring real work, and automation wired to act on both. Put them together and the system stops being something you maintain and starts being something that maintains itself.</p>

<h1>Part Three — The Zero Ops journey: the art of the possible</h1>
<p>Now the fun part. Here’s what each rung actually feels like, across the scenarios teams really run.</p>
<h3>Crawl — the agent suggests, you do the work</h3>
<p>You’ve connected context and you’re asking questions. It’s already useful: <em>“Which of these 40 alerts overnight actually mattered?”</em> — and it tells you, with reasoning.</p>
<p>At this rung the <strong>governance sweep</strong> produces its first report: here are your idle resources, here are the network rules that don’t match policy, here are the CVEs you’re exposed to. Just a list — but it’s a list nobody had time to produce before, and it took four minutes.</p>
<p>The <strong>certificate scan</strong> tells you what expires in the next 90 days. The <strong>cost analysis</strong> names your top ten spenders and why they moved. The <strong>change reviewer</strong> reads an incoming change request and tells you, in plain language, what it actually touches and what depends on it — the blast-radius analysis somebody used to do by hand in a change advisory board meeting. You still do all the work. But for the first time, you can see everything.</p>
<h3>Walk — the agent does the work, one step at a time</h3>
<p>Now it acts, asking before each step. This is where investigation and root-cause analysis come alive.</p>
<p>An alert fires and the agent has already pulled the telemetry, correlated the recent deployment, checked the dependency, and posted a probable cause on the incident — before the on-call has finished reading the title. The <strong>question responder</strong> starts answering “is the EU region healthy?” in your team channel, with evidence.</p>
<p>The governance sweep grows a spine: it doesn’t just list the orphaned resources, it <strong>recommends</strong> what to do about each. The CVE report becomes a prioritised remediation plan. The change reviewer stops describing the change and starts <strong>drafting</strong> it — the implementation plan, the validation steps, and the rollback procedure, written before anyone approves anything.</p>
<p>You approve every step. It feels slow. It is also where you discover exactly what your agent is good at — and every gap you find becomes tomorrow’s artifact.</p>
<h3>Run — the agent completes whole tasks; you review the change</h3>
<p>Triggers are wired now — incidents, webhooks, schedules — and work starts without you. <strong>This is the rung where the 3am page stops arriving.</strong></p>
<p>The <strong>alert-class handler</strong> takes a whole class end to end: fires on arrival, investigates, applies the safe mitigation — restart, scale up, roll back the release — documents it, resolves it. You read about it in the morning.</p>
<p>This is the rung where the word <strong>self-healing</strong> finally earns its place. It’s worth being precise about what it means, because it’s a phrase that gets stretched: self-healing is when the agent <em>detects a known failure class, decides on the response, and acts on it within bounds you pre-approved.</em> Not “the agent does whatever it thinks best.” The class is chosen by you. The safe actions are enumerated by you. The agent’s contribution is that it does the work at 3am, correctly, without waking anyone — and tells you exactly what it did.</p>
<p>And notice that this is granted <strong>per alert class, never per service</strong>. It’s completely normal for one fleet to run some classes at near-total autonomy while other classes sit at a deliberate zero, because nobody’s ready yet. That’s not inconsistency. That’s the control working.</p>
<p>The <strong>capacity agent</strong> sees the quota curve heading for a wall and raises it before anything breaks. The <strong>certificate agent</strong> opens the renewal PR on schedule. The <strong>maintenance agent</strong> handles the planned work that used to eat somebody’s weekend — the scheduled patching round, the index rebuild, the node pool rotation — running it in the window, verifying it landed, and reporting on it. The <strong>change agent</strong> executes the approved change in non-production, validates it, and raises the pull request and the change record together. The <strong>governance sweep</strong> stops recommending and starts <strong>acting</strong> — opening pull requests against your infrastructure-as-code to close the findings it used to just report. The CVE backlog that only ever grew? It starts going <em>down</em>, because something is working it every single day.</p>
<p>And the <strong>work-item loop</strong> appears: a backlog item goes in, a custom agent writes the code and opens a pull request. You review the diff.</p>
<p>Which is exactly when you meet the review wall.</p>
<h3>Fly — the agent proves the outcome, and improves the system</h3>
<p>Fly is not “the agent can execute.” It’s two much better things.</p>
<p><strong>Fly, part one: the agent can prove the outcome is correct.</strong> It builds the fix. It deploys it to a test environment. It runs the validation itself — synthetic checks, browser tests, the full suite. Then it posts the evidence.</p>
<p>You stop reviewing the diff and start reviewing <strong>the outcome</strong>. That’s how the wall comes down.</p>
<p>Now the work-item loop closes completely: backlog item → code → deploy → tested → evidence posted. The <strong>release-safety agent</strong> doesn’t just roll back after an incident, it gates the deploy beforehand — validating in test and blocking the bad one. The governance sweep pushes its own fix to production, having proven in test that it works. And your <strong>standard changes</strong> — the well-understood, pre-approved, thousand-times-executed ones — get carried out in production end to end, validated, and the change record closed with the evidence attached. The change advisory board stops reviewing procedure and starts reviewing outcomes, which is what it always wanted to be doing.</p>
<p><strong>Fly, part two: the agent improves the system.</strong> It learns from every incident. It improves knowledge, artifacts, runbooks, skills — and its own custom agents. The <strong>alert-quality loop</strong> turns inward: it notices which of your alerts are chronic false positives and opens PRs to fix the alert rules themselves. Your monitoring gets better while you sleep.</p>
<p>The system gets better without a human editing it.</p>
<h3>And back to where we started</h3>
<p>That 3am page? A whole class of them doesn’t reach a person anymore.</p>
<p>The fortnight-long cost review? A standing job that finds the waste and opens the PR.</p>
<p>The zero-day marathon? The agent maps exposure across every service in minutes, patches in test, proves it works, and hands you evidence.</p>
<p>The CVE backlog that only grew? Something works it every day, and it shrinks.</p>
<p><strong>That’s Zero Ops.</strong> Not zero humans — zero <em>operations</em> for humans. Your people set intent, govern the system, and validate outcomes. Everything below that line takes care of itself.</p>
