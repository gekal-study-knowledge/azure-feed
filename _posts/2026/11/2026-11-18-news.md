<p><strong><span><span>By Sudha Mahajan, Partner PM, Azure Networking, and Pankaj Garg, CVP, Azure Networking</span></span><span>&nbsp;</span></strong></p>
<p><span>In August, we introduced the public preview of Azure Multicloud Interconnect with Amazon Web Services (AWS) and described a simple goal: making the network the easiest part of a multicloud strategy, not the hardest. The preview replaced much of the customer-assembled work across circuits, routers, Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) sessions, encryption, and provider coordination with one logical resource and an integrated activation workflow.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Today, that journey advances in three important ways. Azure Multicloud Interconnect with AWS is now generally available. We are also announcing the public preview of Azure Multicloud Interconnect with Google Cloud, extending the same managed connectivity model to another cloud provider. And we are working with Lumen to simplify last mile connectivity into Azure, helping customers connect on-premises locations to Azure through a more integrated ExpressRoute and carrier experience.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>Together, these announcements move Azure networking toward a consistent model across cloud-to-cloud and site-to-cloud connectivity: private paths, resilient infrastructure, provider-coordinated provisioning, and clear operational ownership, while customers retain control of their virtual networks, routing and security policies, and application architecture.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<h4><span><span>The problem: distributed applications, fragmented connectivity</span></span><span>&nbsp;</span></h4>
<p><span>Enterprises use multiple environments for practical reasons: distributed data and AI services, regulatory requirements, phased migrations, disaster recovery, mergers, partner ecosystems, and branch connectivity.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span>The challenge is creating a private, resilient, supportable, and end-to-end service. When customers must integrate across cloud and network providers themselves, provisioning can be slow, configuration problems difficult to isolate, and operational ownership</span><span> is</span><span> unclear.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>

Redundant paths across physically separate locations reduce exposure to an isolated device, link, or site failure, while the providers coordinate the lifecycle of the managed connection.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>

### From recordings to insights: Azure Bastion brings AI to privileged session review

- **Link**: [https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/from-recordings-to-insights-azure-bastion-brings-ai-to/ba-p/4573409](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/from-recordings-to-insights-azure-bastion-brings-ai-to/ba-p/4573409)
- **Published**: 2026-11-18 03:41:56
- **Fetched**: 2026-11-18 03:49:32

<details class="entry-summary">
<summary>詳細を表示</summary>
<p>A privileged session can last an hour. A risky action can take seconds.</p>
<p>For security teams, finding those seconds has often meant watching the entire recording. Multiply that effort across administrators, virtual machines, and daily operations, and even a small or midsize enterprise can accumulate more footage than its teams can reasonably review.</p>
<p>The evidence exists. The challenge is turning it into understanding.</p>
<p>At Microsoft Ignite, we are announcing the public preview of <strong>RDP vision, SSH vision, and SSH command logs in Azure Bastion</strong>. These capabilities bring AI-assisted session review and detailed command visibility to privileged access, helping customers understand what happened and identify activity that deserves closer attention.</p>
<p>Together, they extend the value of session recording: from preserving administrative activity to helping security teams make sense of it.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>The challenge: recording sessions is only the beginning</strong></p>
<p>Privileged access is essential to keeping systems running. Administrators troubleshoot applications, update configurations, manage permissions, and maintain critical infrastructure. Those same privileges can also enable actions with significant security consequences.</p>
<p>Azure Bastion already supports graphical session recording for both RDP and SSH through its Premium offering, preserving a visual record of activity for review. <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/bastion/session-recording" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Learn more about Azure Bastion session recording</a>.</p>
<p>But a recording does not explain itself.</p>
<p>To understand a session, a reviewer may need to follow desktop interactions, interpret terminal activity, and distinguish routine administration from actions that warrant investigation. When a question arises about a particular change, finding the relevant activity can require substantial playback and analysis.</p>
<p>This is not a challenge reserved for the largest organizations. A lean IT team managing a modest environment can generate a considerable volume of recordings through everyday maintenance and support. The time needed to review those sessions competes directly with the time needed to operate and protect the business.</p>
<p>Customers need a more scalable way to answer fundamental questions: What happened during this session? Which actions deserve attention? Where should an investigator look next?</p>
<img />
<p><strong>Introducing a clearer view of privileged activity</strong></p>
<p>The new Azure Bastion capabilities address this challenge through complementary forms of visibility.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>RDP vision: understand activity beyond the connection.</strong></p>
<p>RDP vision brings AI-assisted analysis to recorded remote desktop sessions. Session summaries help reviewers understand administrative activity, while insights into noteworthy and potentially risky actions help focus follow-up.</p>
<p>Instead of beginning every review with uninterrupted playback, teams can start with a concise account of the session and use the recording to examine the underlying context.</p>
<p>This is particularly valuable for desktop-based administration, where understanding an action may depend on interpreting a sequence of visual interactions.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>SSH vision: bring context to terminal sessions.</strong></p>
<p>SSH vision applies AI-assisted review to recorded SSH sessions, helping teams understand terminal activity through session summaries and insights into noteworthy and potentially risky actions.</p>
<p>A terminal recording can contain extensive activity. SSH vision helps reviewers establish what took place and decide which sessions merit deeper investigation, reducing the effort required to build that initial understanding.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>SSH command logs: examine commands directly.</strong></p>
<p>SSH command logs complement the visual recording and AI-assisted insights with a detailed record of SSH commands.</p>
<p>For reviewers investigating command activity, this provides a more direct way to examine what was entered. Teams can use command detail alongside the session summary and recording to assess administrative actions in context.</p>
<p>Each capability contributes a different perspective: a summary to orient the reviewer, activity insights to guide attention, and command detail to support deeper examination.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>A more focused investigation workflow</strong></p>
<p>Consider a security team investigating an unexpected configuration change.</p>
<p>With recordings alone, the team may need to watch multiple sessions to determine which contain relevant activity. Longer sessions add review time, even when most of the activity is routine.</p>
<p>With the new capabilities, reviewers can begin with RDP vision or SSH vision summaries to understand the sessions under investigation. They can examine surfaced activity to prioritize follow-up and, for SSH sessions, inspect command logs for additional detail. The underlying recordings remain available to validate findings and understand the broader sequence of events.</p>
<p>This changes the starting point of an investigation. Reviewers begin with context that helps guide their work.</p>
<p>AI-assisted insights support human judgment. Security teams assess the evidence, interpret business context, and decide what action to take.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Security innovation for the AI era</strong></p>
<p>As organizations adopt AI and accelerate the pace of their operations, security teams need ways to manage growing volumes of activity without a corresponding increase in manual review.</p>
<p>Azure Bastion applies AI to a concrete customer problem: understanding privileged sessions at scale.</p>
<p>The innovation lies in bringing together visual session evidence, AI-assisted interpretation across RDP and SSH, and detailed SSH command logs within the Bastion recording workflow. This combination helps customers connect secure administrative access with clearer oversight of what happens during that access.</p>
<p>Built on Azure Bastion Premium session recording, with recordings stored in the customer’s designated Azure Storage account, these capabilities extend an established foundation with a new layer of insight.</p>
<p>For customers, the value is practical: less effort spent establishing what happened, more focused investigation of activity that matters, and better use of the evidence they already capture.</p>
<p>At Ignite, we are taking the next step in privileged access security – helping customers move from recording sessions to understanding them.</p>
<p><strong>Explore </strong><a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/bastion/bastion-overview" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong>Azure Bastion</strong></a><strong> and evaluate how RDP vision, SSH vision, and SSH command logs can support your privileged-session review needs.</strong></p>

### Introducing Agent Grid

- **Link**: [https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/introducing-agent-grid/ba-p/4573407](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-networking-blog/introducing-agent-grid/ba-p/4573407)
- **Published**: 2026-11-18 03:42:08
- **Fetched**: 2026-11-18 03:49:32

<details class="entry-summary">
<summary>詳細を表示</summary>
<p>A single request to an AI agent can set an entire network in motion.</p>
<p>To prepare a customer briefing, an agent might consult a large language model, delegate research to another agent, invoke an MCP tool, query a platform service, retrieve records from a SaaS application, and search the internet. Multiple such hops combine into a single business transaction.</p>
<img />
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Agentic traffic is a web of interactions:</strong> Agent to model, agent to agent, agent to MCP server, agent to PaaS, agent to SaaS, and agent to internet. Each connection introduces a security decision. Together, they create a challenge that grows with every agent, tool, and delegation.</p>
<p>Where should enterprises enforce security across this web? Deploying another gateway for every interaction multiplies infrastructure, routing complexity, and policies to maintain. Consolidating everything behind a single gateway can introduce a bottleneck and a single point of failure. A gateway-by-gateway approach becomes increasingly difficult to scale as workflows cross hosting environments and trust boundaries.</p>
<img />
<p>At Microsoft Ignite, we are announcing&nbsp;<strong>Agent Grid: Identity-based, Zero Trust secure network access for agents.</strong> Agent Grid brings network isolation, micro-segmentation, inspection, and granular Layer 3–7 policies into Azure’s network infrastructure. Its context- and content-aware controls help enterprises govern agent communications across supported environments, without modifying agent code or managing sidecars.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Agents are untrusted insiders</strong></p>
<p>Agents operate inside enterprise environments with legitimate access to sensitive information and powerful tools. Yet malicious content, compromised dependencies, or unexpected model behavior can redirect their actions.</p>
<p>The security premise must therefore be clear: <strong>Agents are untrusted insiders.</strong> Their access needs enforceable boundaries that remain effective when an agent behaves unsafely.</p>
<p><a href="https://x.com/satyanadella/status/2108931348857827686" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Satya Nadella’s call for stronger AI containment</a> reflects this principle: assume a model could be compromised and build independent controls to contain its actions.</p>
<p>A recent, publicly documented research incident illustrates why. During a frontier-model training run, an internal model exploited a DNS resolver misconfiguration to obtain limited access to the live internet, crossing an intended containment boundary. In a separate incident, AI agents reportedly attempted to attack or penetrate a public model-sharing platform – an episode that indirectly echoed concerns associated with major open-source AI hubs. The incidents were contained, and training and evaluation of the most capable models were paused while defenses were strengthened.</p>
<p>Subsequent controlled testing uncovered additional weaknesses, including ways to escape isolated execution environments and use permitted infrastructure services as indirect paths to external systems. These adversarial research settings differ from customer production deployments, but the architectural lesson applies: Containment requires overlapping execution, network, and monitoring controls.</p>
<p>For enterprises, this means <strong>placing security enforcement outside the agent’s trust boundary</strong>. A compromised agent should not control the rules governing its own communications.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>The customer challenge is bigger than connectivity</strong></p>
<p>Enterprises need agents to reach the resources required to complete their work. But permission to connect does not establish permission to perform every available operation.</p>
<p>A trusted MCP server might expose both a search tool and a destructive delete operation. An approved domain might host both necessary APIs and restricted endpoints. A legitimate support ticket might carry malicious instructions to export customer records.</p>
<p>These interactions also span heterogeneous environments. An agent on Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS) might delegate work to an agent in Azure Container Instances (ACI), with both accessing models, enterprise services, and external tools.</p>
<p>Security teams need to establish which workload is communicating, constrain its permitted destinations and operations, inspect relevant content, and understand enforcement outcomes across those environments. When suspicious activity emerges, they need a way to contain affected workloads without depending on the agents themselves.</p>
<p>Separate controls for every runtime and communication path make this difficult. Customers need a common security model that follows workload identity and understands agent interactions.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Zero Trust network access for the agentic enterprise</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid brings these requirements together in a shared network security layer across supported Azure substrates.</p>
<p><strong>Network isolation and micro-segmentation</strong> constrain which agents, tools, and services can communicate. Identity-based policies establish explicit boundaries between workloads, helping limit lateral movement and unnecessary access.</p>
<p><strong>Context-aware enforcement</strong> uses verified workload identity, destination, protocol, and the requested MCP tool operation to evaluate communications. <strong>Content-aware inspection</strong> examines supported application traffic and integrates prompt-injection protection.</p>
<p>Together, these capabilities give enterprises granular control across Layers 3–7, from network access to application and tool interactions.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Transparent, agent-aware inspection</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid provides network-level security and Layer 3–7 visibility into interactions among agents, MCP servers, models, and external services.</p>
<p>Enforcement operates in Azure’s network infrastructure, outside the agent’s sandbox. Customers can apply supported inspection and filtering policies without changing agent code or deploying customer-managed sidecars.</p>
<p>This makes security an infrastructure capability that administrators can manage independently of agent development.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Granular policies and MCP tool governance</strong></p>
<p>Trusting an MCP server should not automatically authorize every tool it exposes.</p>
<p>Agent Grid inspects MCP protocol interactions and enforces tool-level allow and block policies. An enterprise can permit an agent to retrieve support records while restricting tools that delete records, modify configurations, or initiate sensitive operations – even on the same trusted server.</p>
<img />
<p>These controls complement authorization at the destination service, adding an independent enforcement boundary for tool use.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<img />
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Consistent policies across substrates</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid provides centralized management of FQDN, URL, and application-layer security policies across onboarded workloads on supported Azure hosting environments.</p>
<img />
<p>Security teams can express common security intent and see enforcement outcomes across environments. Customers can choose an execution model for its isolation and operational requirements while maintaining consistent network protection.</p>
<img />
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Verified identity and secure connectivity</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid uses SPIFFE standards and SPIRE-based attestation to cryptographically establish workload identity for all workloads including agents and MCP servers. Attestation verifies workload authenticity and provenance against configured trust criteria.</p>
<p>Verified identities support granular access policies and mutually authenticated, encrypted connectivity using mutual TLS (mTLS), independent of IP addresses or the underlying compute substrate.</p>
<p>Where TLS inspection is configured, authorized inspection becomes an explicit part of the protected path: traffic is decrypted for policy evaluation and re-encrypted onward, using platform provided managed-certificates.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Observability and visibility across agent interactions</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid provides a centralized view of agent communications through live inspection logs as well as an interactive flow map, connecting workload identities with the agents, MCP servers, models, and external destinations they access. Security teams can distinguish allowed and denied requests, examine communication patterns, and filter activity by time, source, destination, and policy verdict to understand how network policies are being enforced.</p>
<p>This visibility helps teams investigate unexpected connections, identify unauthorized access attempts, and refine policies based on observed behavior. Integration with Azure Monitor and Log Analytics supports deeper investigation and alerting, while retained flow records preserve visibility into short-lived workloads after they leave the grid.</p>
<img />
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>What we are announcing at Ignite</strong></p>
<p>The Agent Grid preview supports three execution options:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ACI Agent Groups:</strong> A new virtualization-based isolation option for agent workloads, with serverless execution through Azure Container Instances.</li>
<li><strong>AKS Agent Sandbox:</strong> Isolated agent execution using Kata Containers, combining virtualization-based boundaries with Kubernetes orchestration.</li>
<li><strong>Native Kubernetes workloads on AKS:</strong> Network protection for onboarded workloads running directly on AKS.</li>
</ul>

<p>These policies provide complementary safeguards. Destination filtering constrains where an agent can communicate. MCP filtering governs which tools it can invoke. Content inspection helps detect malicious instructions within supported traffic.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>How it works</strong></p>
<p>Agent Grid combines centralized governance with security embedded across supported Azure hosting environments. A regional control plane manages policies and SPIRE-based workload identity, while platform-integrated network brokers capture agent traffic outside the agent’s trust boundary, without code changes or customer-managed sidecars. The brokers establish mutually authenticated, encrypted connections using mTLS and route traffic into a grid-scoped security boundary, where managed proxies, policy engines, and content inspection services enforce network access, segmentation, and granular application and MCP tool policies. This architecture delivers consistent protection across hosting environments, with protection services that scale independently of agent compute.</p>
<img />
<p>Agent Grid separates workload execution, security administration, and traffic enforcement through three architectural layers.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong> Connect and protect workloads outside their trust boundary.</strong><br />An Azure-managed network enforcement component connects onboarded workloads to Agent Grid. It operates outside the agent’s sandbox, establishes attested workload identity, and applies network access and traffic-redirection policies without requiring application changes or customer-managed sidecars.</li>
<li><strong> Define and coordinate policy centrally.</strong><br />A managed control plane coordinates workload enrollment, identities, and policy distribution. Administrators define isolation, micro-segmentation, destination access, and inspection requirements centrally, with enforcement applied across participating environments.</li>
<li><strong> Inspect and enforce in managed protection services.</strong><br />Traffic requiring inspection passes through Azure-managed protection services over authenticated, encrypted connections. These services evaluate supported application traffic, MCP tool calls, and content against configured policies, then allow or block the interaction and provide enforcement visibility.</li>
</ol>
<p>Protection services scale independently of agent compute. Distributed enforcement close to workloads, combined with centrally managed policies and scalable inspection, addresses the operational burden of assembling a separate gateway deployment for every agent interaction.</p>
<p>The architectural principle is straightforward: <strong>Agents execute tasks; infrastructure enforces their communication boundaries.</strong></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>

<p>AI is widening that imbalance. Defenders need protection that can understand emerging threats, recognize where they matter, and act while patching is still underway.</p>
<p>Today at Microsoft Ignite, we are announcing <strong>Azure Adaptive Virtual Patching</strong>, an AI-based adaptive shield designed to proactively reduce exposure to new, emerging, and zero-day vulnerabilities. It connects vulnerability intelligence and workload context with coordinated protection <strong>across the network and on the workload</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Turning the adaptive security vision into protection</strong></p>
<p>In his August 2026 blog, <a href="https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/the-patch-window-is-collapsing-why-security-needs-a-new-control-plane/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">“The patch window is collapsing: Why security needs a new control plane,”</a> Igor Sakhnov, Corporate Vice President and General Manager for Azure Networking, described a growing customer challenge: knowing that a workload is vulnerable does not mean an organization can immediately patch it. He outlined the opportunity for the network to serve as an adaptive control plane that reduces exposure during that interval.</p>
<p>Azure Adaptive Virtual Patching advances that vision into a new product capability.</p>
<p>It connects an understanding of how vulnerabilities work with an understanding of the workloads customers actually run. AI then orchestrates targeted mitigations through network-based controls, including inline intelligence, and eBPF-based runtime enforcement on the workload.</p>
<p>Together, these capabilities bring protection to multiple points along an attack path while customers prepare the permanent fix.</p>

<p><strong>The attack timeline has changed. Production responsibilities have not.</strong></p>

AI accelerates research, code development, and iteration across the attack lifecycle.</p>
<p>Attackers can also access <em>special models</em>: models modified to weaken or remove safety refusal behavior. Research has found that models fine-tuned to remove safeguards can provide substantially more assistance with malicious tasks, further lowering barriers to misuse.</p>
<p>These capabilities can help adversaries move from vulnerability information to attack development faster than customers can responsibly test and deploy a fix.</p>
<p>A production change still requires validation. A critical service still has availability commitments. A maintenance window still needs coordination.</p>
<p>Customers should not have to choose between rushing an untested patch into production and leaving a known attack surface exposed.</p>
<p>Meeting this challenge requires AI-orchestrated defense that continuously connects emerging threats to customer environments – and translates that understanding into protection.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Three semantic layers. One adaptive defense.</strong></p>
<p>Azure Adaptive Virtual Patching uses three connected semantic layers to move from vulnerability understanding to workload-specific mitigation.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong> Understand the vulnerability.</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>The first layer builds a semantic knowledge graph of vulnerabilities, including emerging and newly disclosed zero-days. It connects affected components, exploit prerequisites, vulnerable behaviors, and potential mitigations.</p>
<p>The objective goes beyond recognizing a vulnerability identifier. It is to understand which software is involved, what conditions an attacker needs, and where controls could interrupt exploitation.</p>
<p>As intelligence develops, that understanding evolves with it.</p>
<ol start="2">
<li><strong> Understand the customer’s Azure environment.</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>The second layer builds a semantic knowledge map of the customer’s Azure estate.</p>
<p>It brings together telemetry about running processes, installed modules, operating systems, open ports, and other workload characteristics to understand how systems are configured and exposed.</p>
<p>The presence of a software package is only part of the picture. Exposure also depends on whether a vulnerable component is running, how it is used, and which network paths make it reachable.</p>
<p>This layer grounds vulnerability intelligence in each workload’s operational context.</p>
<ol start="3">
<li><strong> Understand exposure – and orchestrate protection.</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>The third layer combines the first two to determine the relevant attack surface and corresponding mitigation.</p>
<p>The service’s AI core reasons across vulnerability prerequisites, workload configuration, and network exposure to orchestrate protection at the appropriate enforcement points. Depending on the vulnerability and workload, this can involve network mitigations, local runtime controls, or a coordinated combination.</p>
<p>Together, the layers connect <strong>what the threat requires, what the customer runs, and where protection can interrupt the attack.</strong></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Protection across the network and on the workload</strong></p>
<p>The adaptive shield brings together two complementary forms of enforcement.</p>
<p><strong>Network-based mitigations use the network as a compensating control.</strong> Multiple network defenses, including inline intelligence, help identify and interrupt exploit-related traffic and constrain the paths through which vulnerable workloads can be reached.</p>
<p>This gives defenders a way to reduce exposure while the underlying software remains unpatched. The network becomes an active protection layer, applying controls around the workload while remediation proceeds.</p>
<p><strong>Workload-based mitigations address relevant runtime behavior.</strong> Using eBPF-based enforcement, the service can apply granular controls at supported execution points on the workload itself.</p>
<p>The AI core coordinates these capabilities according to the vulnerability and the customer’s environment. A network control may constrain an incoming attack path, while a workload policy addresses the runtime operation that makes exploitation possible.</p>
<p>This combination expands the opportunities to interrupt an attack and tailor protection to the exposed resource.</p>
<p><strong>From protected resource to protected execution</strong></p>
<p>Customers bring workloads under the adaptive shield by adding them as <strong>protected resources</strong>. A <strong>neuron extension</strong> is then installed on each onboarded workload, connecting that resource to the service’s AI core.</p>
<p>The extension collects telemetry that helps the AI core understand the workload’s exposure and attack surface. It also deploys workload mitigations as instructed by the AI core.</p>
<img />
<p>Alongside those local mitigations, the service orchestrates network-based protection to address the relevant exposure paths.</p>
<p>This creates a continuous feedback loop: telemetry informs understanding, understanding informs mitigation, and coordinated enforcement puts protection into effect across the network and workload.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>AI-synthesized policies. Granular runtime enforcement.</strong></p>
<p>On the workload, mitigations are deployed through <strong>Tetragon</strong>, using eBPF-based security across user-mode and kernel-mode execution paths as needed and supported.</p>
<p><a href="https://tetragon.io/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Tetragon</a> provides security observability and runtime enforcement, including visibility into process execution, system calls, and file and network activity. Its <a href="https://tetragon.io/docs/reference/tracing-policy/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">policy framework</a> supports hooks in kernel and user-space code, allowing policies to target specific operations and take defined actions when their conditions match.</p>
<p>The AI core synthesizes Tetragon policies dynamically, making them highly specific to each workload’s software, configuration, and relevant exploit conditions. The neuron extension deploys those policies to the corresponding protected resource.</p>
<p>Two workloads affected by the same vulnerability may run different binaries, load different modules, or expose different network paths. Their protections should reflect those differences.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>AI adaptability with deterministic protection</strong></p>
<p>Production security demands predictable, reliable behavior.</p>
<p>A central innovation in Azure Adaptive Virtual Patching is bringing AI-driven reasoning together with deterministic enforcement. AI interprets vulnerability information and workload context, then translates that understanding into defined mitigation controls.</p>
<p>For workload protection, synthesized Tetragon policies express explicit matching conditions and enforcement actions. Once deployed, those policies enforce the defined behavior locally without requiring a new generative AI decision for every matching event.</p>
<p>Across the Adaptive Shield, the principle is consistent: <strong>AI determines the appropriate protection; defined controls make enforcement predictable.</strong></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Autonomous preparation for emerging threats</strong></p>
<p>When actionable intelligence about a newly announced zero-day becomes available, AI-based training pipelines can trigger autonomously to prepare defenses at scale.</p>
<p>The pipelines develop an understanding of exploit conditions and candidate protections. The semantic layers connect that learning to customer environments, and the AI core prepares workload-specific mitigations across the available network and runtime enforcement points.</p>
<p>The result is a continuous cycle: understand the threat, assess exposure, prepare mitigation, enforce protection, and adapt as intelligence or workload conditions change.</p>
<p>Proactive, pre-emptive defense means acting on those conditions before an attack succeeds.</p>

<p>The original research describes header compression amplification combined with stalled streams. Small requests can cause disproportionate server memory consumption, while flow-control behavior keeps resources occupied. Request size alone does not capture the risk: header counts, allocation behavior, and stream lifetime also matter.</p>
<p><strong>In our testing, Azure Adaptive Virtual Patching successfully mitigated HTTP/2 Bomb attacks.</strong></p>

<p>That context informs where protection should apply. Network-based mitigations can address exploit-related traffic at an appropriate inspection point, while workload enforcement can target relevant runtime behavior. The service orchestrates the applicable controls for the workload.</p>

<p>The tested outcome demonstrates the value of coordinated protection. PHP’s advisories also make clear that interim measures do not provide complete coverage across every exploitation route. Protection against tested attacks buys time; installing the fixed software remains essential.</p>

<p><em>By Jay Soni (Senior Product Manager) and Jordi Rey (Product Manager), Azure Application Gateway</em></p>
<p>Modern applications change quickly. Teams run A/B experiments, version their APIs, tailor experiences by client and tenant, and serve AI models across reserved and pay-as-you-go capacity. Azure Application Gateway now gives you more precise control over where each request goes and how traffic is distributed once it gets there.</p>
<p>Two new capabilities are now in public preview. Use them on their own or together:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Advanced routing rules:</strong> Match requests on path, header, query string, HTTP method and client IP, with regular expression support. A single rule can now cover request patterns that previously needed several.</li>
<li><strong>Advanced load balancing:</strong> Choose backend pools by weight or priority, then choose servers within each pool using round robin, weighted round robin or least connections.</li>
</ul>
<p>The rest of this post explains how these capabilities work and the scenarios they enable, from blue-green deployments and client-aware experiences to multi-tenant SaaS and a single entry point for your AI endpoints.</p>
<h2>What's new</h2>
<div class="styles_lia-table-wrapper__h6Xo9 styles_table-responsive__MW0lN"><table border="1" style="width: 100%; border-width: 1px;"><thead><tr><th>Capability</th><th>Details</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>What a rule can match</td><td>Path, HTTP header, query string, HTTP method and client IP</td></tr><tr><td>How values are matched</td><td>Exact values, or RE2 regular expressions for path, header and query string</td></tr><tr><td>How conditions combine</td><td>Up to five conditions per rule, joined with AND and evaluated in priority order</td></tr><tr><td>Load balancing across pools</td><td>Multiple pools per rule, selected by weight or by priority</td></tr><tr><td>Load balancing within a pool</td><td>Round robin, weighted round robin or least connections, set per pool</td></tr><tr><td>Failover between pools</td><td>Priority-based failover driven by a configurable Minimum Healthy Percent</td></tr></tbody><colgroup><col style="width: 50.00%;" /><col style="width: 50.00%;" /></colgroup></table></div>
<h2>Advanced routing rules: more control over request routing</h2>
<p>Define conditions on URL paths, HTTP headers, query strings, HTTP methods and client IP addresses to send each request to the right backend pool. Combine conditions in one rule to support scenarios such as API versioning, tenant-specific routing and feature-based deployments. Rules are evaluated in priority order, and the first matching rule determines the destination. Requests that don't match any rule go to the configured default backend pool.</p>
<p>Path, header and query-string conditions support RE2 regular expressions, so a single pattern can match many request variations. For example, <code>^/api/orders(/.*)?$</code> matches <code>/api/orders</code> and all of its subpaths. Each rule can also apply a rewrite rule set as part of routing.</p>
<h2>Advanced load balancing: traffic distribution that fits your workload</h2>
<p>Advanced load balancing gives you control at two levels:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Across pools:</strong> Use weighted distribution to split traffic by relative weight, such as 80/20 for a gradual rollout. Or use priority to keep traffic on a primary pool and fail over to the next pool when the primary pool's healthy servers drop below your Minimum Healthy Percent.</li>
<li><strong>Within a pool:</strong> Each pool uses its own algorithm. Round robin spreads requests evenly. Weighted round robin sends more requests to larger servers. Least connections sends each request to the healthy server with the fewest active connections, so a server still working through long-running requests receives less new traffic.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Scenarios you can build</h2>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">1. Canary and blue-green releases</h3>
<p>Route requests that carry an <code>x-release: canary</code> header to a new version, or send 10% of all traffic to the green pool and raise its weight as confidence grows. Taking a pool out of rotation is a weight change, not a redeployment, and your customer-facing endpoint never changes.</p>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">2. Read/write split</h3>
<p>Send GET requests to a pool of read replicas or cache-heavy nodes, and send POST, PUT and DELETE requests to the primary write tier.</p>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">3. Client-aware experiences</h3>
<p>Route mobile clients to an optimized backend based on a client header such as <code>x-client-type: mobile</code> or a User-Agent pattern, while web traffic continues to the standard pool.</p>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">4. Internal and partner traffic</h3>
<p>Route requests from corporate or partner IP ranges to dedicated backend pools, such as staging or administrative environments. Combine client IP-based routing with Web Application Firewall (WAF) protection and appropriate authentication and authorization controls to help secure access to these applications.</p>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">5. Multi-tenant SaaS</h3>
<p>Route by tenant hostname to dedicated pools for premium tenants and shared pools for everyone else. Tenant tiers become a routing decision, not an infrastructure redesign.</p>
<h3 class="lia-indent-padding-left-30px">6. API versioning without URL changes</h3>
<p>Route <code>?api-version=2026-01</code> or an <code>api-version</code> header to the right backend. Clients keep a stable URL while you run several versions side by side, and one regular expression condition can cover a whole family of versioned paths.</p>
<h2>One entry point for your AI endpoints</h2>
<p>AI applications often span several endpoints, mixing reserved Provisioned Throughput Unit (PTU) capacity and pay-as-you-go capacity across regions. Rather than having every application track which endpoint to call and what to do when reserved capacity runs out, Application Gateway can serve as the central entry point for all of them: one address, with TLS, WAF and private connectivity managed in one place.</p>
<p><strong>Route by deployment, spill over automatically.</strong> Route AI requests to the appropriate backend pools based on deployment paths, team identifiers or application-specific headers. Put PTU endpoints in a priority 1 pool and pay-as-you-go endpoints in a priority 2 pool. Traffic moves to pay-as-you-go only when the share of healthy reserved endpoints drops below your Minimum Healthy Percent, with no change for the client.</p>
