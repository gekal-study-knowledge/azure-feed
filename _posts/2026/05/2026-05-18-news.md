<h1>A meshed hybrid hub-spoke topology</h1>
<p>Azure offers two main approaches to build network architectures. This article focuses on <strong>traditional networking</strong> (using VNets, peering, route tables, etc.), rather than Azure Virtual WAN.</p>
<h2>Why a hub-spoke topology?</h2>
<p>A hub‑spoke topology is the only way to <strong>control traffic flows while maintaining scalability</strong>, because it enforces a central point of connectivity and policy enforcement:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Centralized traffic control / inspection: </strong>All connectivity (to on‑premises, the internet, and between spokes) is anchored through the hub. The hub hosts shared services such as firewalls or NVAs, providing a single control point where traffic is inspected, filtered, and governed consistently.</li>
<li><strong>Avoids uncontrolled lateral communication: </strong>Spokes do not connect arbitrarily to each other. All connectivity is routed through the hub, preventing uncontrolled east‑west communication and ensuring traffic follows defined security and routing policies.</li>
<li><strong>Inherent scalability by design: </strong>New workloads are added by introducing additional spokes. The core network design remains unchanged, enabling linear scaling without the complexity of full-mesh connectivity.</li>
</ul>
<p>In summary, the hub‑spoke model provides centralized control combined with scalable, decoupled workload networks—something that flat or full-mesh designs struggle to achieve.</p>
<h2>From hub-spoke to meshed multi-region</h2>
<p>In a hub‑spoke topology, it’s important to keep in mind that the hub is implemented as an Azure Virtual Network (VNet) and VNets are scoped to a single region. This means that in a multi‑region setup, you’ll always need at least one hub per region. Each of these hubs hosts shared services like firewalls, NVAs, and DNS, acting as the central point for connectivity and traffic control.</p>
<p>Extending dependencies across regions—for example by connecting spokes to a hub in another region—is generally not recommended. It creates tight coupling between regions, which goes against the goal of keeping regions independent. A well-designed multi‑region architecture aims for <strong>regional self‑containment to improve resilience and fault isolation</strong>. Relying on a remote hub can lead to issues like failure propagation between regions, higher latency for inspected traffic and more complex routing and operations. It can also introduce organizational challenges when different regions are managed by separate teams, reducing agility and increasing operational risk.</p>
<p>For this reason, meshed hub‑spoke architectures should use <strong>hubs that are deployed within each region</strong>. Connectivity between regions should be established directly between the hubs, not through spokes. In a meshed design, hubs are typically connected in a full‑mesh peering model, allowing for controlled and predictable inter‑region communication while still maintaining regional independence.</p>
<p>Within a single region, it can also make sense to deploy multiple hubs to create isolated environments. This is especially useful when you need to separate workloads based on security requirements, regulatory needs, or organizational boundaries. Each hub can then have its own dedicated set of connectivity and inspection services.</p>
<p>Finally, <strong>each spoke VNet connects to just one hub</strong>. This keeps routing simple and predictable, ensures that all traffic passes through the correct inspection and policy enforcement layers, and reinforces the hub’s role as the central control point for network traffic within the region.</p>
<h2>Integrating hybrid connectivity</h2>
<p>In most enterprise scenarios, Azure doesn’t operate in isolation—it needs to connect to <strong>external networks</strong> such as on‑premises datacenters or other cloud environments. This hybrid connectivity is typically set up using services like Azure ExpressRoute, Azure VPN Gateway or third‑party SD‑WAN solutions. In a (meshed) hub‑spoke topology, these<strong> connectivity components are best deployed in the hub VNet</strong>, since the hub acts as the central point where all inbound and outbound traffic comes together.</p>
<p>By centralizing external connectivity in the hub, all traffic—whether entering or leaving Azure—can be routed, inspected and governed in a consistent way using shared services like firewalls or NVAs. It also avoids the need to duplicate gateways and connectivity components across multiple spokes, which helps reduce cost and operational overhead.</p>
<p>This approach also simplifies routing and policy management. Spokes can rely on the hub’s shared connectivity instead of maintaining their own connections to external networks. Overall, this reinforces the hub’s role as the single, controlled integration point between Azure and the broader network landscape.</p>
<h1>Implementation fundamentals</h1>
<p>With the overall architecture in place, the next step is to understand how Azure actually handles routing and traffic control in this kind of design.</p>
<p>When working with a hub‑spoke topology in Azure, it’s important to realize that a virtual network (VNet) doesn’t behave like a traditional router. While you can associate <strong>Azure Route Tables</strong> with subnets, those <strong>routes only apply to traffic originating from within that subnet</strong>. Traffic entering the VNet from outside isn’t automatically re‑routed. This is also why VNet peering is non‑transitive by design: peered VNets can communicate directly, but they won’t forward traffic for other networks.</p>
<p>To enable controlled routing between spokes—and between Azure and external networks such as ExpressRoute or VPN—you need a component in the hub that can actively receive and forward traffic. In most cases, this is handled by an Azure Firewall or a network virtual appliance (NVA) deployed in the hub. These components act as an explicit routing hop: they receive traffic, inspect or process it based on defined policies and then send it back into the virtual network so Azure’s routing engine can continue forwarding it.</p>
<p>In a <strong>secure</strong> hub‑spoke design, the <strong>firewall plays a dual role</strong>. It not only provides centralized traffic inspection and enforces security policies, but also acts as the mechanism that enables transitive communication between spokes and external networks. This combination of control and connectivity is a key part of the architecture. Of course, this only works as intended if the firewall is configured with the right rules to allow or block traffic according to your security requirements.</p>
<p>While it’s technically possible to implement routing using a basic virtual machine or even a Virtual Network Gateway, these approaches don’t meet typical enterprise requirements. They lack built‑in capabilities like advanced traffic inspection, high availability, autoscaling and centralized policy management. Purpose‑built solutions such as Azure Firewall or mature third‑party NVAs are designed to provide not just routing, but also integrated security, consistency, and scalability. For that reason, they’re generally the only realistic choice for production‑grade hub‑spoke environments where both control and resilience matter.</p>
<h1>Design principles for building the topology</h1>
<p>The diagram below shows the topology for a hybrid meshed hub-spoke, with 2 hubs and an Azure Firewall (any other 3rd party Firewall could be used as well).</p>
<img />
<p>Ensuring correct connectivity in a hub-and-spoke topology may initially appear complex, but in practice it comes down to understanding and correctly applying <strong>four key design principles</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>controlled routing in the GatewaySubnet</li>
<li>controlled routing in each spoke</li>
<li>proper peering of spokes to the hub</li>
<li>meshing the hubs.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before looking at these in detail, it is important to understand a fundamental behavior of Azure Virtual Network (VNet) peering. When two VNets are peered, Azure automatically <strong>exchanges their address spaces (CIDR ranges) and injects these prefixes as system routes into the effective route tables of all subnets.</strong> As a result, resources in one VNet can communicate directly with resources in the other using private IP addressing, without any additional routing configuration. This built-in route propagation is what makes VNet peering an efficient and low-latency connectivity mechanism in Azure.</p>
<p>However, this default behavior is not always aligned with the requirements of a hub-and-spoke topology. In this model, network services such as firewalls, inspection and routing control are typically centralized in the hub VNet. If communication between spokes is allowed to follow the automatically injected system routes, traffic could bypass these centralized controls, which would undermine design objectives such as inspection, segmentation and governance.</p>
<p>For this reason, although VNet peering provides seamless connectivity by default, additional configuration is required in a hub-and-spoke architecture. This is usually achieved through <strong>Azure Route Tables, network virtual appliances (NVAs) or Azure Firewall</strong>, ensuring that traffic between spokes is routed through the hub as intended. This approach enables a controlled routing model that is <strong>essential for maintaining security and architectural consistency</strong> in enterprise-scale Azure environments.</p>
<h2>Design principle 1: Controlled routing in the GatewaySubnet</h2>
<p>In hybrid connectivity scenarios, traffic originating from on-premises environments over VPN or ExpressRoute is first terminated by the Azure Virtual Network Gateway. From there, the traffic is injected into the Azure network using the routing context of the GatewaySubnet. By default, this process <strong>relies on system routes that are automatically populated through VNet peering</strong>. As a result, when the destination resides in a spoke VNet, the traffic is forwarded directly to that spoke, since its address space has already been learned and installed as a system route. While this behavior is efficient, it also means that traffic will bypass centralized security controls in the hub, such as Azure Firewall.</p>
<p>To ensure that all incoming traffic is properly inspected, this <strong>default routing behavior needs to be adjusted</strong>. This is done by <strong>associating a custom Azure Route Table with the GatewaySubnet and defining user-defined routes for each spoke address range</strong>. These routes should point to the private IP address of the firewall as the next hop, effectively overriding the system routes created by VNet peering. Because Azure gives precedence to user-defined routes over system routes, traffic that would normally go directly to the spoke is instead redirected through the firewall before reaching its destination.</p>
<p><strong>It is important that these user-defined routes precisely match the CIDR ranges defined for the spoke VNets!</strong> Any mismatch, such as using broader or more specific prefixes, can lead to unexpected routing behavior and may introduce issues such as asymmetric traffic flows or packet loss. For instance, if a spoke uses address spaces like 10.10.10.0/24 and 192.168.10.0/24, these exact prefixes must be reflected in the route table. Only by aligning the custom routes with the advertised address ranges can you ensure predictable routing and consistent inspection through the firewall.</p>
<p><strong>If the hub VNet hosts additional resources</strong> beyond an Azure Firewall or third-party network virtual appliance that also require traffic inspection, <strong>the corresponding CIDR ranges</strong>—either for the specific subnets or for the entire hub VNet—<strong>should be included as routes in the route table associated with the GatewaySubnet</strong>. These routes should be configured in the same way as those for spoke VNets, ensuring that traffic destined for these resources is directed through the intended inspection point. A typical example is Azure DNS Private Resolver, which can include both inbound and outbound endpoints deployed in dedicated subnets. When such endpoints are present in the hub, their associated subnet address ranges must also be added to the route table for the GatewaySubnet. This ensures that traffic to and from these endpoints is routed through the designated inspection path, maintaining consistent enforcement of security controls.</p>
<h2>Design principle 2: Controlled routing in every spoke</h2>
<p>In a hub-and-spoke architecture, traffic flows should follow the intended security model. <strong>Workloads within the same spoke VNet</strong> are usually treated as part of the <strong>same trust boundary</strong>, so traffic between resources in that spoke can flow directly over the Azure backbone without needing to pass through centralized controls. <strong>Network Security Groups (NSGs)</strong> should still be used at the subnet level to provide granular, stateful filtering, but routing this traffic through a central firewall is typically not required.</p>
<p>The situation changes when traffic leaves the local VNet. As soon as <strong>traffic is destined for another spoke, the hub, or on-premises networks</strong>, it <strong>crosses a trust boundary and needs to be inspected</strong> centrally. To enforce this, Azure’s default routing behavior must be overridden by associating an <strong>Azure Route Table</strong> with each subnet in the spoke VNets.</p>
<p>In most cases, this route table can be kept simple by defining a <strong>single default route</strong> that sends all outbound, non-local traffic to the firewall in the hub:</p>
<ul>
<li>Destination: 0.0.0.0/0</li>
<li>Next hop: Private IP address of the hub firewall (Virtual Appliance)</li>
</ul>
<p>With this configuration in place, all traffic that is not local to the spoke is forced through the hub, ensuring that communication between VNets and towards external networks is inspected and controlled.</p>
<p>From a management perspective, the same route table can often be reused across multiple subnets or even multiple VNets within the same subscription, which helps keep the design consistent and easy to maintain. It’s worth noting, however, that Azure requires route tables and the subnets they’re associated with to be in the same subscription, as this association is enforced by the platform.</p>
<img />
<p>There is one additional setting that is often overlooked but plays an important role in getting routing right in a hub-and-spoke design. Azure route tables include an option called <strong>“Propagate gateway routes”</strong>, which controls whether routes learned by a Virtual Network Gateway are added to the effective routes of the associated subnets. By default, routes learned via BGP (for example from ExpressRoute or VPN) or defined through a Local Network Gateway are propagated not only within the hub VNet, but also across VNet peerings. This means that spoke VNets can automatically learn routes to on-premises or external networks and may send traffic directly to the gateway, bypassing the firewall in the hub.</p>
<p>To avoid this and keep traffic flowing through the centralized security controls, this setting should be disabled on the route tables used by the spoke subnets. When <strong>“Propagate gateway routes”</strong> is set to <strong>No</strong>, routes learned by the gateway are no longer injected into the spokes. As a result, traffic to those destinations cannot take a direct path and instead follows the user-defined default route (0.0.0.0/0) toward the hub firewall, where it can be properly inspected.</p>
<p>When combined with the default route to the firewall, this setup ensures that traffic—whether it is going to other VNets, on-premises environments, or external networks—always follows a controlled and predictable path through the hub. This helps maintain consistent security enforcement and avoids unexpected routing behavior in larger or hybrid deployments.</p>
<img />
<h2>Design principle 3: Peering the spokes to the hub</h2>
<p>Virtual Network (VNet) peering in Azure is often seen as a simple, single configuration, but in reality it is <strong>directional by design</strong>. To fully connect two VNets, you need <strong>two separate peering configurations</strong>—one in each direction—and both must be configured correctly to ensure not only connectivity, but also proper routing behavior. Each peering exposes <strong>four key settings </strong>&nbsp;and getting these right is especially important in a hub-and-spoke architecture.</p>
<p>For basic connectivity, the first two settings—<strong>“allow virtual network access”</strong> and <strong>“allow forwarded traffic”</strong>—should be enabled on both peerings. These ensure that traffic can flow between VNets and support scenarios where traffic is routed through a central component, such as a firewall in the hub. The other two settings depend on the direction of the peering. In a typical hub-and-spoke setup, the <strong>Virtual Network Gateway (or Azure Route Server)</strong> is deployed in the hub. This means the peering from the spoke to the hub must enable <strong>“use remote gateways”</strong>, while the peering from the hub to the spoke must enable <strong>“allow gateway transit.”</strong></p>
<p>At first, this might seem to contradict the idea that spokes should not directly use the gateway. However, these settings influence <strong>control plane behavior and </strong>don't enable unrestricted traffic flow. They are required so the gateway can&nbsp;<strong>learn and advertise spoke address ranges via BGP</strong> to external networks, such as those connected over VPN or ExpressRoute. Whether those routes are actually used in the spokes is still controlled through the <strong>“propagate gateway routes”</strong> setting on the route tables, allowing you to enforce routing through the firewall as intended.</p>
<p>Even if you are not currently using BGP—for example, in environments relying on static routing—it is still a good practice to configure peerings this way. Doing so makes the design <strong>future-proof</strong>, allowing you to introduce dynamic routing later without changes to the peering model. This approach keeps the architecture consistent and avoids unnecessary rework as the environment evolves.</p>

<h2>Design principle 4: Meshing the hubs</h2>
<p>When you extend a hub-and-spoke design across multiple regions, you typically introduce multiple hubs, each managing its own regional spokes. In this setup, it becomes important to connect the hubs to each other, which is done by <strong>fully meshing the hub VNets using VNet peering</strong>. At the same time, a key principle remains unchanged: each spoke should connect to <strong>only one hub</strong> <strong>in the same region</strong>. This keeps the architecture simple, scalable and easier to reason about from a routing perspective.</p>
<p>When configuring connectivity between hubs, it’s important to note that <strong>VNet peering settings differ from the typical hub–spoke configuration</strong>. For inter-hub peerings, only <strong>“allow virtual network access”</strong> and <strong>“allow forwarded traffic”</strong> should be enabled. The remaining options—<strong>“allow gateway transit”</strong> and <strong>“use remote gateways”</strong>—should be left disabled, as gateway sharing is not required between hubs and would even be blocked in the configuration.</p>
<p>Just connecting the hubs with peering is not enough to guarantee correct traffic flow. To ensure traffic moves between regions in a controlled and secure way, you need additional routing logic. Each hub should have an Azure Route Table assigned to its&nbsp;<strong>FirewallSubnet (or the subnet hosting the 3rd party NVAs)</strong>&nbsp;defining how traffic towards other hub-and-spoke environments is handled. This ensures that inter-region traffic is <strong>always routed through the appropriate hub firewall</strong>, instead of flowing directly across the Azure backbone.</p>
<p>At this point, <strong>IP address planning becomes critical</strong>. Without a clear addressing strategy, routing quickly becomes complex and hard to maintain. A common best practice is to assign a <strong>single “master” CIDR range per region</strong>, and then allocate all VNets in that region—both hub and spokes—from that range. This creates a clean, hierarchical addressing model that simplifies routing decisions.</p>
<p>With this approach in place, route tables can remain relatively simple. Instead of adding routes for every individual spoke, you only need <strong>one route per remote region</strong>. The destination is the <strong>master CIDR range of that region</strong>&nbsp;and the next hop is the <strong>private IP of the firewall in the corresponding hub</strong>. Because all hubs are peered with each other, these address ranges and firewall endpoints are automatically known through peering, allowing for consistent and predictable routing.</p>
<p>Overall, this design keeps routing logic straightforward while ensuring that <strong>all inter-region traffic is inspected in the correct hub</strong>, preserving the security model and making it easy to scale as new regions are added.</p>
<h1>Conclusion</h1>
<p>When the four design principles described in this article are applied consistently, a hub-and-spoke architecture becomes a strong, scalable and easy-to-operate foundation for your network. By combining controlled routing, centralized inspection and clear traffic flows, the model delivers both solid security and predictable behavior, even in complex environments.</p>
<p>More importantly, the concepts covered here go beyond just one specific design. They represent the key building blocks of Azure networking, including routing, peering and traffic control. Understanding these fundamentals not only helps you implement hub-and-spoke topologies correctly, but also gives you a solid base for designing and running reliable, enterprise-grade network architectures in Azure.</p>
